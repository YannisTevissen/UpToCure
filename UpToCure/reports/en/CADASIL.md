# CADASIL

## Overview

CADASIL (cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy) is an inherited disorder of the brain’s small blood vessels caused mainly by disease-causing variants in the *NOTCH3* gene. It is usually passed from an affected parent to a child in an autosomal-dominant pattern, meaning each child has a 50% chance of inheriting the variant. Symptoms commonly begin in adulthood and can include migraine with aura, recurrent transient ischemic attacks or strokes, mood or psychiatric symptoms, progressive problems with thinking, and eventually vascular dementia. Severity and timing vary substantially, including according to the location of the *NOTCH3* variant. [GeneReviews: CADASIL](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1500/)

CADASIL is progressive, but its course is not uniform: some people remain mildly affected for decades, while others develop repeated strokes, loss of independence, and dementia in mid- to later adulthood. There is currently no approved therapy proven to prevent CADASIL progression, stroke, or vascular dementia. Standard care focuses on managing stroke and migraine symptoms, rehabilitation and cognitive or psychiatric support, control of blood-pressure and other vascular risk factors, and individualized decisions about antiplatelet or anticoagulant medicines. [GeneReviews: CADASIL](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1500/)

## Scope of Recent Research (2020–present)

Research activity has increased meaningfully since 2020, particularly around the idea that CADASIL is driven not simply by loss of normal NOTCH3 function but by toxic accumulation of mutant and normal NOTCH3 extracellular protein in vessel walls. The leading cure-oriented questions are whether researchers can remove those deposits, correct the inherited variant in vascular cells, or preserve the survival and function of cerebral vascular smooth-muscle cells; however, the field remains preclinical overall, with no curative therapy yet in human trials. [Protein aggregates drive arterial pathology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38386425/) [GeneReviews: CADASIL](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1500/)

## Major Breakthroughs and Emerging Therapies

**Immunotherapy to clear NOTCH3 deposits** is the most mature disease-modifying strategy. In a CADASIL mouse model, active vaccination with aggregates containing mutant and normal NOTCH3 extracellular-domain fragments reduced NOTCH3 deposition around brain capillaries by 38%–48%, lowered circulating NOTCH3 extracellular-domain levels, and did not produce detected retinal vascular-cell loss, neurodegeneration, kidney injury, or overt inflammation during the study. This is important because it directly attacks a defining pathological feature rather than only treating stroke consequences; nonetheless, it remains an animal result, not evidence of benefit in people. [Active NOTCH3 immunotherapy in mice](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9906330/)

The biological rationale for removing deposits strengthened in 2024. A mouse study found that aggregates containing both mutant and wild-type NOTCH3 were major drivers of arterial smooth-muscle-cell loss and arterial pathology, while many NOTCH3-regulated genes remained largely unchanged. This supports therapeutic approaches that selectively reduce pathogenic extracellular NOTCH3 accumulation while preserving essential NOTCH3 signaling in blood vessels. [Protein aggregates drive arterial pathology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38386425/)

**Gene correction** made an important proof-of-concept advance. Researchers created induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)—adult patient cells reprogrammed into stem-cell-like cells—from a person carrying the *NOTCH3* p.R421C variant, corrected that variant with an adenine base editor, and showed that corrected iPSC-derived vascular smooth-muscle cells no longer displayed the NOTCH3 deposition and abnormal cytoskeletal features seen in the uncorrected cells. A dual adeno-associated virus (AAV) delivery system achieved average editing of 8.82% in laboratory-grown blood-vessel organoids. This demonstrates that precise repair is technically possible, but the editing level, delivery challenge, off-target safety, immune response, and need to reach widespread brain vessel cells make this far from a clinical cure. [Base editing in CADASIL iPSCs and vessel organoids](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37164272/)

**Repurposed vascular-rescue approaches** are also emerging. In a patient-derived iPSC model designed to resemble brain-specific vascular smooth-muscle cells, CADASIL variants impaired nitric-oxide signaling, cell–matrix attachment, contractility, and cell survival. The phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor sildenafil and a nitric-oxide donor reversed several cellular abnormalities and improved survival in vitro. Sildenafil is already approved for other conditions, which may ease future clinical testing, but this result does not yet establish that it can clear NOTCH3 deposits, prevent strokes, or alter CADASIL progression in patients. [PDE5 inhibition in CADASIL iPSC models](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13048245/)

## Clinical Trials and Experimental Approaches

The principal recent interventional study is **AMCAD (NCT06072118)**, a completed multicenter, investigator-initiated, single-arm Phase II trial sponsored by Japan’s National Cerebral and Cardiovascular Center. It enrolled a planned 60 genetically confirmed CADASIL participants to receive intravenous adrenomedullin—a vasoactive peptide with proposed blood-vessel, anti-inflammatory, and white-matter protective effects—for eight hours daily over 14 days. Its primary endpoint was change in frontal-cortex cerebral blood flow at day 28 measured with arterial-spin-labeling MRI; the registry lists the study as completed but states that no results have been posted. [AMCAD trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06072118?rank=4&term=AREA%5BConditionSearch%5D%28%22Dementia%2C+Multi-Infarct%22%29) [AMCAD Phase II protocol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10875187/)

AMCAD is a disease-modification attempt rather than a genetic cure: adrenomedullin is intended to improve the neurovascular unit—the interacting blood-vessel, glial-cell, and white-matter system—rather than repair *NOTCH3*. Other major current studies are observational but are essential for future cure trials. For example, the Korean K-CADASIL registry, sponsored by Jeju National University Hospital, aims to follow approximately 500 participants for 10 years with clinical assessments, MRI, genetic data, and a genomic/proteomic biorepository. [K-CADASIL registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07497867)

## Methodologies and Scientific Approaches

Researchers increasingly combine animal models with human, patient-specific platforms. CADASIL mouse models are used to test whether therapies can reduce vascular NOTCH3 deposits and preserve vessel function, while patient-derived iPSCs can be differentiated into vascular smooth-muscle cells, endothelial cells, astrocytes, neurons, and three-dimensional vessel organoids. These human systems have shown impaired blood–brain-barrier support and abnormal neurovascular interactions, providing more relevant platforms for gene editing and drug screening. [iPSC neurovascular-unit model](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37361999/) [Base editing in CADASIL iPSCs and vessel organoids](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37164272/)

Biomarker work is becoming more trial-ready. Researchers are using MRI measures such as white-matter injury, brain volume, microbleeds, and cerebral blood flow, alongside blood neurofilament light-chain levels and skin-biopsy measurements of vascular NOTCH3 accumulation. An international 2025 study developed a nine-stage NOTCH3-associated small-vessel-disease system that was validated across 1,713 individuals from 15 countries and linked stages with imaging, cognition, blood neurofilament light, and long-term survival. [NOTCH3-SVD severity staging](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39610302/) [NOTCH3 aggregation and disease severity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40265482/)

## Strengths, Limitations, and Challenges

The strongest recent progress is that multiple approaches now target plausible root mechanisms: immunotherapy can reduce NOTCH3 deposition in mice, base editing can correct a disease variant in patient-derived cells, and human vascular models can reveal drug-responsive defects. The main advantage of CADASIL as a therapeutic target is that it is caused by a single gene, enabling precise genetic and molecular strategies. [Active NOTCH3 immunotherapy in mice](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9906330/) [Base editing in CADASIL iPSCs and vessel organoids](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37164272/)

The limitations are substantial. CADASIL variants are numerous, so a mutation-specific editing therapy may not serve all families; AAV-based delivery would need to reach large numbers of vascular cells throughout the brain; and permanently altering *NOTCH3* or broadly inhibiting NOTCH-related pathways could damage normal vessel maintenance. In addition, CADASIL progresses slowly and variably, making clinical trials long, expensive, and dependent on sensitive biomarkers that can detect meaningful benefit before strokes or dementia occur. [GeneReviews: CADASIL](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1500/) [NOTCH3-SVD severity staging](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39610302/)

## Outlook and Future Directions

As of August 8, 2026, CADASIL is not close to a proven clinical cure, but the path toward one is clearer than it was in 2020. The milestones to watch are publication of AMCAD outcomes; confirmation that anti-NOTCH3 immunotherapy restores vessel function and prevents neurological injury in additional models; demonstration that gene editors can safely and efficiently reach brain vascular cells in vivo; and validation of MRI, blood, and skin biomarkers that can support practical early-stage human trials. A realistic nearer-term goal is a disease-modifying treatment that slows vascular injury and stroke risk; a durable cure would likely require safe, broad correction or silencing of the pathogenic *NOTCH3* effect before irreversible vessel and brain damage occurs.
