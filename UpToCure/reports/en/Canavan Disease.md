# Canavan Disease

## Overview

Canavan disease is a rare inherited leukodystrophy, meaning a disorder that damages the brain’s white matter. It is caused by harmful variants in both copies of the *ASPA* gene, which normally makes aspartoacylase, an enzyme that helps break down the brain molecule N-acetylaspartate (NAA). Without sufficient enzyme activity, NAA builds up and disrupts myelin, the insulation around nerve fibers. The typical, more severe form begins at about three to five months of age with poor head control, low muscle tone, developmental delay, progressive loss of skills, enlarged head size, visual impairment, and later spasticity and seizures; a smaller group has a milder, more variable course. Life expectancy is reduced, although outcomes vary substantially and some people with atypical disease reach adulthood. [Canavan Disease](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1234/)

There is currently no approved cure. Standard care is multidisciplinary and supportive: seizure and spasticity management, physical and occupational therapy, nutrition and swallowing support, respiratory and infection-risk monitoring, mobility equipment, developmental services, and palliative or respite support when needed. [Canavan Disease](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1234/)

## Scope of Recent Research (2020–present)

The field is small but notably active for an ultra-rare disorder, with research concentrated on replacing the missing *ASPA* gene, lowering excess NAA, improving delivery throughout the brain, and establishing biomarkers and natural-history data that can make very small clinical trials interpretable. Two phase 1/2 AAV gene-replacement programs are now or recently have been in clinical development, and early human data provide evidence of biological activity, but the evidence is still insufficient to call any approach curative. [Canavan Disease](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1234/) [Oligodendrocyte-targeted AAV trial](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03919-w)

## Major Breakthroughs and Emerging Therapies

The leading strategy is *ASPA* gene replacement using adeno-associated virus (AAV), a modified virus used to deliver a functional gene. In 2025, an open-label phase 1/2 study reported 12-month interim data for MYR-101, an rAAV-Olig001 vector designed to preferentially reach oligodendrocytes, the myelin-producing brain cells most relevant to Canavan disease. Eight children received a single intracranial dose. Cerebrospinal-fluid NAA fell, MRI-based myelination increased, and developmental scores improved; all reported adverse events and serious adverse events resolved, and none of the serious events was judged related to MYR-101. These findings are encouraging but come from a small, uncontrolled early-stage study. [Oligodendrocyte-targeted AAV trial](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03919-w)

A second gene-replacement strategy uses AAV9, a vector capable of broader central-nervous-system delivery after intravenous administration. The CANaspire study of BBP-812 delivers human *ASPA* using AAV9, while a separate 2023 expanded-access report described one child treated with rAAV9-CB6-*ASPA* through both intravenous and intracerebroventricular routes, together with immune suppression. In that single-patient report, NAA fell and remained stable through four years of follow-up, while MRI myelination and motor measures improved; however, one-person expanded-access experience cannot establish effectiveness or predict safety across the broader Canavan population. [CANaspire trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04998396) [Dual-route AAV9 expanded-access study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10432950/)

A complementary preclinical strategy addresses both sides of the disease mechanism: supplying *ASPA* while reducing production of NAA by silencing neuronal *NAT8L*, the gene that encodes the NAA-producing enzyme. In symptomatic *Aspa*-deficient mice, a dual-function AAV vector reduced NAA and reversed multiple disease features, including vacuolation, demyelination, abnormal astrocyte activation, impaired movement, and weight loss. This is important because it suggests a way to widen the treatment window after symptoms have begun, but it remains an animal-model result rather than a human therapy. [Dual-function AAV therapy in mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36568275/)

## Clinical Trials and Experimental Approaches

MYR-101, developed by Myrtelle, is being evaluated in the phase 1/2 CAN-GT study (NCT04833907) as a single intracerebroventricular administration of rAAV-Olig001-*ASPA* in children with typical Canavan disease. The published interim analysis of eight participants found lower cerebrospinal-fluid NAA, increased myelination, and improved developmental outcomes at 12 months, but longer follow-up and comparison with well-characterized untreated natural history remain necessary. [CAN-GT registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04833907) [Oligodendrocyte-targeted AAV trial](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03919-w)

Aspa Therapeutics/BridgeBio’s CANaspire phase 1/2 study (NCT04998396) is evaluating intravenous AAV9-based BBP-812 in pediatric participants. The latest GeneReviews update describes the trial as recruiting and reports preliminary reductions in urine, cerebrospinal-fluid, and brain NAA, improved MRI myelination, and early favorable motor trends; these sponsor-associated preliminary findings should not be interpreted as confirmatory clinical efficacy until fuller peer-reviewed results and longer-term safety data are available. [CANaspire trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04998396) [Canavan Disease](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1234/)

## Methodologies and Scientific Approaches

Researchers combine *Aspa*-deficient mouse models, cultured neurons, vector-engineering experiments, and human natural-history cohorts to test whether a treatment restores ASPA activity, lowers NAA, protects white matter, and improves function. The major delivery comparison is between direct brain or ventricular injection—which can target the central nervous system more directly—and systemic AAV9 administration, which may produce wider distribution but introduces different dosing and immune-management challenges. [Dual-function AAV therapy in mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36568275/) [Dual-route AAV9 expanded-access study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10432950/)

Biomarkers are central to these studies. NAA can be measured in urine, cerebrospinal fluid, and brain magnetic-resonance spectroscopy, while MRI-based myelin measures, developmental testing, and gross-motor assessments provide complementary evidence of biological and functional change. A 2025 study found that urine NAA distinguished typical from milder Canavan phenotypes, supporting its use for patient stratification and potentially for monitoring treatment response. [Urine NAA phenotyping study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1089/hum.2024.168) [Oligodendrocyte-targeted AAV trial](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03919-w)

## Strengths, Limitations, and Challenges

The strongest feature of current research is that it targets the root biology: replacing the missing enzyme and measuring whether NAA falls and myelin improves. The MYR-101 study provides the clearest peer-reviewed multi-patient evidence to date that gene delivery can change relevant biochemical, imaging, and developmental measures, while the dual-function mouse work offers a plausible approach for patients treated after pathology is established. [Oligodendrocyte-targeted AAV trial](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03919-w) [Dual-function AAV therapy in mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36568275/)

The central limitations are the very small patient population, lack of randomized controls in early studies, variable disease severity, irreversible injury that may precede treatment, and uncertainty about how durable AAV expression will be in the developing brain. Direct brain delivery requires neurosurgery, whereas systemic delivery requires careful management of immune responses and dose-related safety. NAA reduction and MRI improvement are meaningful pharmacodynamic signals, but researchers must still demonstrate durable gains in survival, communication, motor function, comfort, and day-to-day quality of life. [Dual-route AAV9 expanded-access study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10432950/) [Canavan Disease](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1234/)

## Outlook and Future Directions

As of August 8, 2026, Canavan disease is closer to a disease-modifying therapy than it was at the start of this decade, but not yet close enough to claim a cure. The milestones to watch are longer-term MYR-101 follow-up, peer-reviewed multi-patient BBP-812 results, confirmation that biomarker improvements translate into durable functional benefit, and preclinical advancement of combined *ASPA* replacement plus *NAT8L* suppression. Earlier diagnosis and treatment—potentially supported by urine, MRI, and newborn-screening-compatible NAA assays—may ultimately matter as much as the vector itself, because preventing white-matter injury is likely easier than reversing longstanding damage.
