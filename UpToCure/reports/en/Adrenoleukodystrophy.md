# Adrenoleukodystrophy

## Overview

Adrenoleukodystrophy (ALD), usually X-linked ALD, is an inherited disorder caused by disease-causing variants in the *ABCD1* gene. The resulting shortage of the ALD protein disrupts breakdown of very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs), which accumulate and contribute to damage in the brain, spinal cord, and adrenal glands. ALD has several unpredictable forms: rapidly inflammatory cerebral ALD (cALD), most often in boys; slowly progressive spinal-cord disease called adrenomyeloneuropathy (AMN), usually in adult men and sometimes women; and adrenal insufficiency. Genotype does not reliably predict which form or severity a person will develop. [International ALD recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9687408/)

Without early treatment, active childhood cALD can progress quickly to major neurological disability or death, whereas AMN commonly causes worsening stiffness, weakness, walking difficulty, bladder dysfunction, and sexual dysfunction over years. Current care combines lifelong surveillance, treatment of adrenal insufficiency with steroid replacement when present, rehabilitation and symptom management for AMN, and rapid referral for hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation (HSCT) or gene therapy when early active cerebral disease is detected. [International ALD recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9687408/) [MRI surveillance guidelines](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8113077/)

## Scope of Recent Research (2020–present)

Research since 2020 has been active but concentrated in a small number of specialist centers and companies. The central questions are how to halt inflammatory brain lesions before irreversible injury, make gene-based treatment safer and more durable, treat the spinal-cord form of ALD, and predict who will develop cerebral disease. A disease-wide cure has not yet been established: the leading approved gene therapy is indicated to slow neurological progression in a narrowly defined group of boys with early active cALD, while AMN and adrenal insufficiency remain untreated by that product. [SKYSONA prescribing information](https://www.fda.gov/media/161640/download) [International ALD recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9687408/)

## Major Breakthroughs and Emerging Therapies

The most consequential advance is autologous lentiviral hematopoietic stem-cell gene therapy, elivaldogene autotemcel (eli-cel; SKYSONA). Doctors collect a patient’s own CD34-positive blood-forming stem cells, insert a functional *ABCD1* complementary DNA copy outside the body using the Lenti-D vector, give chemotherapy conditioning, and infuse the corrected cells back. The intent is for corrected blood-derived cells to enter the brain’s immune-cell system and supply functional ALD protein, thereby reducing inflammation and demyelination. [SKYSONA prescribing information](https://www.fda.gov/media/161640/download)

In the completed phase 2–3 eli-cel study, 32 boys with early-stage active cALD received treatment; 29 completed 24 months without a major functional disability, and overall survival at 24 months was 94%. At a median follow-up of six years, neurological-function scores were stable versus baseline in 30 of 32 participants and 26 participants had no major functional disability. These results represent meaningful disease modification for early cALD, rather than proof that all manifestations of ALD have been cured. [Lentiviral gene therapy for cALD](https://www.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/NEJMoa2400442)

A separate, much earlier-stage strategy is direct injection of an *ABCD1*-carrying lentiviral vector into the brain. In a 2024 phase I study in seven children with cALD, investigators reported no injection-related serious adverse event or death during one year of follow-up, detectable ALD protein in circulating immune cells, and preliminary signals of activity. This was a small, uncontrolled safety study, so it cannot yet establish clinical effectiveness or a cure. [Intracerebral LV-*ABCD1* phase I study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39025777/)

Small-molecule approaches aim to suppress inflammation, protect nerve cells, or reduce harmful lipid accumulation rather than replace *ABCD1* directly. Leriglitazone, an oral brain-penetrant PPAR-gamma agonist, did not improve its primary walking endpoint in the randomized ADVANCE phase 2–3 trial in adult men with AMN, although all six cases of new progressive cALD occurred in the placebo group. [ADVANCE leriglitazone trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36681445/) In the open-label pediatric NEXUS study, an interim analysis of 11 evaluable boys with cerebral lesions found lesion-growth deceleration in all participants at 24 weeks and arrested disease in five, but the study remains too small and uncontrolled to demonstrate that leriglitazone can replace HSCT or gene therapy. [NEXUS interim analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40510922/)

Gene editing and metabolic substrate-reduction research remain preclinical. CRISPR-based homology-independent targeted integration restored *ABCD1* expression and improved VLCFA-related measures in mouse experiments, while adenine base editing corrected a disease-causing *ABCD1* variant and rescued biochemical abnormalities in a humanized mouse model. [In vivo targeted integration study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8753287/) [Adenine base-editing study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11286820/) Separately, Sanofi researchers reported that inhibiting ELOVL1, an enzyme involved in synthesizing VLCFAs, lowered VLCFAs in the brain and spinal cord of *Abcd1*-deficient mice; however, unexpected cellular stress-response changes underline why this is not ready for human use. [ELOVL1 inhibition in ALD mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40894896/)

## Clinical Trials and Experimental Approaches

The pivotal eli-cel program included the phase 2–3 STARBEAM study, NCT01896102, sponsored by bluebird bio, which enrolled 32 participants and completed in 2021; treated patients continue in long-term follow-up. [STARBEAM trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01896102) The U.S. Food and Drug Administration continues to list SKYSONA for boys aged 4–17 years with early active cALD who do not have an available HLA-matched allogeneic donor, under accelerated approval based on 24-month major-functional-disability-free survival. [FDA SKYSONA page](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/skysona)

Leriglitazone is being tested across ALD phenotypes. The NEXUS pediatric phase 2–3 study generated encouraging but preliminary open-label results in childhood cALD. [NEXUS interim analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40510922/) The CALYX trial, NCT05819866, is a randomized, triple-masked phase 3 study sponsored by Minoryx Therapeutics in adult men with cALD; its primary outcome is time to death or becoming bedridden with permanent ventilatory support, and the registry estimates primary completion in May 2027. [CALYX trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05819866)

## Methodologies and Scientific Approaches

ALD investigators combine longitudinal MRI, neurological scoring, adrenal testing, VLCFA assays, and blood or cerebrospinal-fluid biomarkers to identify the narrow treatment window before cerebral lesions cause disabling injury. MRI surveillance is particularly central because brain lesions can appear before clear neurological symptoms; consensus guidance recommends scheduled imaging during childhood for boys at risk. [MRI surveillance guidelines](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8113077/) Researchers are also evaluating quantitative MRI and inflammatory biomarkers to measure whether gene therapy or drug treatment is altering lesion biology earlier than conventional clinical endpoints can show. [CSF and plasma biomarkers after gene therapy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13148947/)

Laboratories increasingly use patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), which can be turned into disease-relevant brain cell types, as well as cortical and spinal-cord organoids. Recent studies generated ALD patient-derived astrocytes and showed that ALD organoids develop elevated VLCFAs and altered levels of brain-relevant lipids, creating platforms for testing gene correction, lipid-lowering compounds, and cell-specific mechanisms. [ALD iPSC-derived astrocytes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40004040/) [ALD cortical and spinal organoids](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41509916/)

## Leading Institutions and Funding

Leading clinical and research centers include Massachusetts General Hospital and Harvard Medical School, Boston Children’s Hospital and Dana-Farber, the University of Minnesota, Kennedy Krieger Institute and Johns Hopkins, Stanford University, Amsterdam UMC, University Hospital Leipzig, and major French and Spanish leukodystrophy centers. These institutions have led the eli-cel, leriglitazone, imaging-surveillance, and natural-history work that underpins current treatment decisions. [Lentiviral gene therapy for cALD](https://www.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/NEJMoa2400442) [International ALD recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9687408/) [NEXUS interim analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40510922)

Industry sponsors include bluebird bio for eli-cel, Minoryx Therapeutics for leriglitazone, and Sanofi for ELOVL1 inhibitor research. Patient-led funding is becoming more prominent: in 2025, ALD Connect launched a Breakthrough Research Fund with a $5 million goal, including a $1 million pledge and a matching commitment for initial donations. [ALD Connect Breakthrough Research Fund](https://aldconnect.org/news/ald-connect-launches-breakthrough-research-fund-to-accelerate-scientific-discovery/) The Will to Cure ALD organization reports more than $3 million raised, $1.2 million committed, and 14 projects funded across six research institutions as of 2026. ## Strengths, Limitations, and Challenges

The field’s main strength is that early cALD is now treatable with approaches that can substantially delay catastrophic neurological decline, especially when surveillance identifies active lesions promptly. Autologous gene therapy avoids donor-cell graft-versus-host disease and provides an option for some boys without a suitable donor. [SKYSONA prescribing information](https://www.fda.gov/media/161640/download) However, treatment remains intensive: it requires stem-cell collection, specialized manufacturing, full myeloablative chemotherapy, hospitalization, and long-term monitoring. [SKYSONA prescribing information](https://www.fda.gov/media/161640/download)

The largest limitation is safety and incomplete coverage of the disease. SKYSONA now carries a boxed warning because myelodysplastic syndrome and acute myeloid leukemia have occurred 14 months to 10 years after treatment, requiring lifelong monitoring; the label also warns that patients with complete *ABCD1* gene deletions may have rapid loss of efficacy through an immune response. [SKYSONA prescribing information](https://www.fda.gov/media/161640/download) Neither eli-cel nor HSCT corrects adrenal insufficiency, and there remains no proven disease-modifying therapy for AMN or for people whose cerebral disease is already advanced. Small sample sizes, external-control comparisons, uncertain long-term durability, limited specialist-center capacity, high cost, and the lack of reliable predictors of cerebral conversion remain major barriers. [SKYSONA prescribing information](https://www.fda.gov/media/161640/download) [International ALD recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9687408/)

## Outlook and Future Directions

ALD is closer to a functional cure for a subset of boys with very early active cALD than it was in 2020, but it is not close to a universal cure. The milestones to watch are long-term eli-cel safety and durability data, especially rates of blood cancers and sustained neurological benefit; completion of randomized leriglitazone studies; validation of biomarkers that predict inflammatory brain conversion; and translation of safer gene-editing or brain-directed delivery systems from animal models into carefully monitored human trials. Progress will depend as much on newborn screening, expert MRI surveillance, and equitable access to transplant and gene-therapy centers as on the next experimental treatment.
