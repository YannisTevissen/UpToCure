# Castleman Disease

## Overview

Castleman disease is a rare group of lymph-node and immune-system disorders marked by abnormal lymph-node growth and, in some forms, excessive inflammatory signaling. It can affect people of any age. **Unicentric Castleman disease (UCD)** involves one lymph-node region and is usually treated—and in most cases cured—by complete surgical removal. **Multicentric Castleman disease (MCD)** affects multiple lymph-node regions and can cause fever, fatigue, weight loss, low blood counts, fluid buildup, kidney dysfunction, and sometimes life-threatening multi-organ failure. MCD includes human herpesvirus-8 (HHV-8)-associated disease and HHV-8-negative/idiopathic MCD (iMCD), with the latter including the particularly severe TAFRO subtype. [UCD consensus guidelines](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7724917/) [iMCD treatment guidelines](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30181172/) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7724917/?utm_source=openai))

For unresectable UCD, treatment is individualized and may include observation, medicine, radiotherapy, or specialized surgery. For iMCD, the standard first treatment is usually an antibody that blocks interleukin-6 (IL-6), a major inflammatory messenger: siltuximab where available, or tocilizumab in settings where siltuximab is unavailable. In the United States, siltuximab is approved for HIV-negative and HHV-8-negative MCD, but it is not a cure and many patients need ongoing treatment or alternative therapies. [FDA orphan-drug record for siltuximab](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/opdlisting/oopd/detailedIndex.cfm?cfgridkey=200605) [iMCD treatment guidelines](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30181172/) ([accessdata.fda.gov](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/opdlisting/oopd/detailedIndex.cfm?cfgridkey=200605&utm_source=openai))

## Scope of Recent Research (2020–present)

Research since 2020 has been active but necessarily small because Castleman disease is rare. The central questions are which cells and molecular pathways initiate the inflammatory state, why many patients do not respond to IL-6 blockade, and whether treatment can produce durable drug-free remission rather than disease control alone. The field has moved beyond an IL-6-only model toward mTOR, JAK-STAT, stromal-cell, vascular, B-cell, and chemokine pathways; however, as of August 8, 2026, no established curative systemic therapy exists for multicentric disease. [mTOR activation study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7205815/) [2025 spatial and single-cell study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40593805/) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7205815/?utm_source=openai))

## Major Breakthroughs and Emerging Therapies

A major therapeutic advance has been the repurposing of **sirolimus**, an oral inhibitor of mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), a signaling hub that regulates immune-cell growth and activation. A 2020 study found increased mTOR-pathway activity in iMCD lymph nodes, blood immune cells, and serum-protein profiles; it also showed that IL-6 stimulation could increase mTOR signaling and that JAK1/2 inhibition could block that effect in laboratory experiments. This provided a biologic rationale for treating patients whose disease persists despite IL-6-directed therapy. [mTOR activation study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7205815/) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7205815/?utm_source=openai))

The mTOR strategy remains promising but is not yet proven to cure iMCD. Research in iMCD-TAFRO found evidence of both mTORC1 and mTORC2 activation in affected lymph nodes, which may help explain why sirolimus, which predominantly suppresses mTORC1, can work well in some people but not uniformly in all patients. More recent work has also identified elevated SVEP1, endothelial activation, clotting abnormalities, complement activation, and angiogenesis-related signals in iMCD-TAFRO, suggesting that vascular and immune-thrombotic biology may eventually yield combination-treatment targets. [mTORC2 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9170805/) [SVEP1 and endotheliopathy study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40191177/) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9170805/?utm_source=openai))

A second emerging strategy is **JAK-STAT inhibition**. JAK proteins transmit signals from cytokines such as IL-6 into immune cells; ruxolitinib is an oral JAK1/2 inhibitor already used in other blood disorders. A University of Pennsylvania phase II study is now evaluating ruxolitinib in previously treated iMCD for people who did not respond to, relapsed after, or cannot take siltuximab or tocilizumab. The trial is an important test of the hypothesis that blocking downstream cytokine signaling can help even when directly blocking IL-6 does not. [Ruxolitinib iMCD trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07085039) [mTOR activation study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7205815/) ([clinicaltrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07085039?utm_source=openai))

Biomarker research is also becoming more actionable. In a 2022 serum-proteomics study of 1,178 proteins, CXCL13—a chemokine that helps organize B-cell immune responses—was markedly elevated in iMCD. A fall of at least 17% in CXCL13 by day 8 after starting siltuximab predicted later response in the study cohort. CXCL13 is not yet an established treatment target, but it could help clinicians identify early whether IL-6 blockade is working and could support development of therapies aimed at the CXCL13–B-cell pathway. [CXCL13 biomarker study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-34873-7) ([nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-34873-7?utm_source=openai))

Finally, researchers are using single-cell and spatial molecular mapping to identify the disease-driving microenvironment within lymph nodes. A 2025 study found abnormal stromal support cells—including follicular dendritic cells, T-zone reticular cells, and perivascular reticular cells—linked to IL-6, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), JAK-STAT, transforming growth factor-beta, and MAPK signaling. This work shifts attention from circulating cytokines alone toward the lymph-node tissue niche that may sustain disease. [2025 spatial and single-cell study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40593805/) ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40593805/))

## Clinical Trials and Experimental Approaches

The University of Pennsylvania’s phase II, open-label sirolimus trial in previously treated iMCD enrolled seven participants and posted results in 2025. The registry reports no deaths during the study period, but one participant experienced serious pneumonia and sepsis. The investigators note that the single-arm design, very small enrollment, short follow-up, COVID-19-related recruitment disruption, and off-label availability of sirolimus prevent firm conclusions about long-term efficacy or safety. [Sirolimus phase II trial results](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03933904?tab=results) ([clinicaltrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03933904?tab=results&utm_source=openai))

Other recent interventional evidence supports treatment escalation rather than cure. In a prospective multicenter study of 24 people with relapsed or refractory iMCD, the bortezomib-cyclophosphamide-dexamethasone regimen produced an overall response in 15 participants (62.5%) at six months; estimated one-year progression-free and overall survival were 79% and 92%, respectively. This chemotherapy-based approach may control severe refractory disease, but its study did not establish that treatment can be safely stopped without relapse. [BCD prospective trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35100929/) ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35100929/?utm_source=openai))

The phase II ruxolitinib study is enrolling adults with previously treated iMCD, with an anticipated enrollment of 14 participants. A separate sponsor-led study of more frequent or higher-dose siltuximab for people who relapsed or became refractory on standard dosing was terminated by the company before results were posted. [Ruxolitinib iMCD trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07085039) [SISREMCAD siltuximab study](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04838860) ([clinicaltrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07085039?utm_source=openai))

## Methodologies and Scientific Approaches

Castleman disease research increasingly combines clinical registries with biospecimen banking, pathology review, blood testing, imaging, proteomics, flow cytometry, and genomic or transcriptomic analysis. The ACCELERATE registry enables patients to contribute longitudinal medical records, laboratory results, surveys, and excess lymph-node tissue, while expert clinicians and hematopathologists review cases to improve diagnostic accuracy and support studies across this very small patient population. [ACCELERATE study design](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7762771/) [ACCELERATE registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02817997) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7762771/?utm_source=openai))

Recent laboratory studies use high-dimensional methods to move from association to therapeutic hypotheses. These include multiplex serum proteomics for biomarkers such as CXCL13, phospho-protein assays to measure active mTOR signaling, single-cell RNA sequencing of circulating immune cells, and spatial transcriptomic and proteomic mapping of lymph-node tissue. These platforms are intended to identify which cell populations produce inflammatory signals and to match future therapies to biological subtypes rather than treating all MCD as one condition. [CXCL13 biomarker study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-34873-7) [single-cell immune-repertoire study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39147879/) [2025 spatial and single-cell study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40593805/) ([nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-34873-7?utm_source=openai))

## Leading Institutions and Funding

The Castleman Disease Collaborative Network (CDCN) is the principal patient-centered research organization in the field. It coordinates international collaborators, supports research grants to nonprofit institutions and universities, recruits patients into research, and prioritizes projects through a strategic network model. CDCN co-founder David Fajgenbaum leads major translational efforts at the University of Pennsylvania, including the sirolimus and ruxolitinib studies and the ACCELERATE registry. ## Strengths, Limitations, and Challenges

The strongest recent progress is the shift toward biologically informed treatment. IL-6 blockade remains effective for a subset of people, while mTOR and JAK inhibition offer rational alternatives for refractory disease. The field now has validated response criteria, an international registry, linked patient samples, and molecular tools that can identify biomarkers and distinct disease pathways. These assets are particularly valuable in a rare disorder where conventional large trials are difficult. [iMCD treatment guidelines](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30181172/) [ACCELERATE study design](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7762771/) [CXCL13 biomarker study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-34873-7) ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30181172/?utm_source=openai))

The limitations are substantial. Castleman disease is biologically heterogeneous, especially across UCD, HHV-8-associated MCD, iMCD-not otherwise specified, and iMCD-TAFRO, so an effective medicine for one subgroup may fail in another. Trials typically enroll very few people, often lack control groups, and have limited ability to establish whether a therapy produces true long-term, drug-free remission. Immunosuppressive strategies can also cause infections and other toxicities, as illustrated by serious infectious events in the small sirolimus trial. Access remains uneven because specialized diagnosis, pathology review, expert care, and approved IL-6-directed therapy are not universally available. ## Outlook and Future Directions

A cure is already realistic for most people with surgically removable UCD, but a reliable systemic cure for multicentric Castleman disease is not yet close. The most meaningful milestones to watch are durable results from the sirolimus study, recruitment and outcomes from the ruxolitinib phase II study, prospective validation of CXCL13-guided treatment decisions, and confirmation that stromal-cell, vascular, or chemokine targets can produce sustained remission after therapy is withdrawn. Progress will depend on integrating registry-based natural-history data with tissue-level molecular profiling so that future trials can test the right therapy in the right biological subtype.
