# Charcot-Marie-Tooth Disease

## Overview

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (CMT) is a group of inherited disorders that damage peripheral nerves—the nerves connecting the brain and spinal cord to muscles and sensory organs. It can cause slowly progressive weakness and wasting in the feet, lower legs, and hands; high-arched feet, foot drop, balance difficulty, numbness, pain, and reduced dexterity. CMT affects people of all sexes and ages, although onset is often in childhood or early adulthood; severity and rate of progression vary greatly by genetic subtype and even within families. [Charcot-Marie-Tooth Hereditary Neuropathy Overview](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1358/)

There is no approved therapy that corrects the underlying cause of CMT. Current care is therefore supportive and individualized, typically combining physiotherapy and exercise, occupational therapy, ankle-foot braces or other orthotics, mobility aids, pain management, foot care, genetic counseling, and orthopedic surgery when deformities substantially impair function. [Charcot-Marie-Tooth Hereditary Neuropathy Overview](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1358/) [Clinical practice guidelines for CMT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38431252/)

## Scope of Recent Research (2020–present)

Research activity has accelerated since 2020, particularly in genetically defined forms of CMT. The central questions are whether treatment can safely correct excess or defective gene activity in peripheral nerves, prevent axon and myelin loss early enough to preserve function, and measure change reliably in a slowly progressive disease. The field is not yet close to a proven cure for CMT as a whole, but several subtype-specific programs now target disease mechanisms rather than symptoms alone, while broader muscle-directed and metabolic treatments are entering or completing early clinical testing. [Developing gene therapy for CMT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40219666/) [Clinical trials in CMT disorders](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10556688/)

## Major Breakthroughs and Emerging Therapies

For CMT1A, the most common subtype, the central therapeutic target is excess peripheral myelin protein 22 (PMP22), caused by duplication of the *PMP22* gene. In mice, an adeno-associated virus (AAV) carrying artificial microRNAs reduced both human *PMP22* and mouse *Pmp22* expression and improved neuropathy features, providing a strong proof of concept for gene silencing. [RNAi gene therapy for CMT1A mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35579942/) Separate work used squalene-linked small interfering RNA (siRNA) nanoparticles to lower *PMP22* in CMT1A mouse models, demonstrating that a nonviral RNA-delivery approach can improve disease-related measures. [PMP22 siRNA nanoparticles](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-021-01839-2) Novartis acquired DTx Pharma and its preclinical DTx-1252 program in 2023; this fatty-acid-conjugated siRNA is designed to reach Schwann cells and reduce excess PMP22. [Novartis acquisition of DTx Pharma](https://www.novartis.com.cn/news/nuohuaxuanbushougou-dtx-pharmaqianghuashenjingkexuechanpinxianhe-xrna-jishupingtainengli)

Gene editing is also being explored for CMT1A. In patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) Schwann-cell precursors, an AAV-delivered CRISPR-associated enzyme reduced the duplicated *PMP22* dosage, normalized PMP22 RNA and protein levels, and improved abnormal cell survival and myelination measures. This is an important laboratory advance, but it remains a cell-based proof of concept rather than a treatment tested in people. [AAV editing of PMP22 in patient-derived Schwann cells](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38017287/)

For recessive CMT forms caused by missing or nonfunctional genes, gene replacement is a more direct strategy. In a CMT4C mouse model, AAV9 delivery of *SH3TC2* specifically to myelinating Schwann cells improved motor performance, nerve conduction, myelin structure, and nodal organization even when treatment was delayed. [AAV9 SH3TC2 gene replacement for CMT4C](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37641403/) In a severe CMT4J mouse model, AAV9-mediated delivery of functional *FIG4* substantially extended survival and improved peripheral-nerve function, with earlier treatment producing more complete rescue. [AAV9 FIG4 gene therapy for CMT4J](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33878035/)

CMT2A, commonly caused by dominant *MFN2* variants, poses a different problem because simply adding a normal gene may not overcome the harmful mutant protein. A 2023 iPSC motor-neuron study combined RNA interference to suppress endogenous *MFN2* with replacement by an RNAi-resistant normal *MFN2* copy; this corrected mitochondrial distribution and mitophagy abnormalities in vitro. [RNA interference and MFN2 replacement for CMT2A](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38007410/) Metabolic precision medicine has also emerged for CMT-SORD: the 2020 discovery that biallelic *SORD* variants raise sorbitol and cause a potentially treatable hereditary neuropathy created a rationale for blocking sorbitol production with aldose-reductase inhibitors. [SORD deficiency discovery](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41588-020-0615-4)

## Clinical Trials and Experimental Approaches

The most advanced recent disease-specific clinical effort in CMT1A was Pharnext’s oral three-drug combination PXT3003. The pivotal randomized Phase III PREMIER trial enrolled 387 people with CMT1A, but topline results reported in December 2023 did not show a statistically significant benefit on its primary disability endpoint; the program illustrates the difficulty of detecting meaningful change over a relatively short period in slowly progressive CMT. [PXT3003 PREMIER Phase III topline results](https://live.euronext.com/en/products/equities/company-news/2023-12-11-pharnext-reports-topline-results-pivotal-phase-iii) Helixmith’s Engensis (VM202), a plasmid DNA treatment encoding hepatocyte growth factor, has also completed a small, open-label Phase I/IIa study in 12 people with CMT1A; its primary aim was safety and tolerability, with exploratory functional, electrophysiologic, and muscle-MRI outcomes. [Engensis CMT1A Phase I/IIa trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05361031)

Several recent trials are mechanism-oriented but should not be described as cures. In CMT-SORD, the randomized Phase II/III INSPIRE trial tested govorestat (AT-007), an aldose-reductase inhibitor intended to lower sorbitol. Results presented in 2025 by the sponsor and patient-advocacy partners reported lower blood sorbitol, slower MRI-measured progression, and favorable functional and patient-reported trends through 18–24 months; these findings require full peer-reviewed publication and regulatory review. [INSPIRE govorestat trial update](https://www.hnf-cure.org/triad/triad-therapeutics/applied-therapeutics/hnf-partner-cmt-sord-clinical-trial-update/) NMD Pharma’s Phase IIa SYNAPSE-CMT trial tested ignaseclant (formerly NMD670), a skeletal-muscle chloride-channel inhibitor, in 81 adults with genetically confirmed CMT1 or CMT2. In February 2026, the company reported that the 21-day trial missed its prespecified six-minute-walk primary endpoint but showed improvements in several secondary strength, dexterity, functional, and patient-reported outcomes; longer, confirmatory studies are needed. [SYNAPSE-CMT ignaseclant topline results](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/02/03/3230913/0/en/NMD-Pharma-announces-topline-results-from-its-Phase-2a-study-of-ignaseclant-in-Charcot-Marie-Tooth-disease-Types-1-and-2.html) ENCell is also testing EN001, a mesenchymal stromal-cell product, in a Phase Ib/IIa CMT1A study with dose-escalation and randomized, placebo-controlled components. [EN001 CMT1A Phase Ib/IIa trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06328712)

## Methodologies and Scientific Approaches

CMT cure research increasingly uses genotype-specific models: transgenic rodents for PMP22 duplication, knockout animals for recessive loss-of-function disorders, and patient-derived iPSCs differentiated into Schwann cells or motor neurons. These models allow researchers to test whether a therapy corrects the relevant biology—such as PMP22 overexpression, defective myelin maintenance, impaired mitochondrial transport, or toxic sorbitol accumulation—before moving into human studies. [RNAi gene therapy for CMT1A mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35579942/) [RNA interference and MFN2 replacement for CMT2A](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38007410/)

A major practical challenge is delivery to the correct peripheral-nerve cell type. Researchers are refining AAV capsids, Schwann-cell-specific gene promoters, intrathecal and systemic administration routes, and lipid or fatty-acid-conjugated RNA platforms. They are also improving trial-readiness measures: quantitative lower-limb muscle MRI fat fraction changed detectably over 12 months in CMT1X, CMT1B, and CMT2A, supporting its use as a biomarker of disease progression and, potentially, treatment response. [AAV9 SH3TC2 gene replacement for CMT4C](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37641403/) [Muscle MRI fat fraction in CMT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10963306/)

## Leading Institutions and Funding

Important academic and clinical contributors include University College London and the UCL Queen Square Institute of Neurology, the University of Rochester, the University of Wisconsin, the Cyprus Institute of Neurology and Genetics, University of Texas Southwestern, the Gladstone Institutes, and international inherited-neuropathy networks. Patient organizations are unusually central to CMT translation: the Charcot-Marie-Tooth Association’s Strategy to Accelerate Research (CMTA-STAR) funds therapeutic projects and coordinates academic-industry collaborations across multiple subtypes. [CMTA-STAR funding process](https://www.cmtausa.org/our-research/for-researchers/funding/)

Representative investments show the scale and breadth of this support. CMTA reports a projected $6.5 million, three-year CMT4C gene-therapy effort intended to advance an AAV program from animal studies toward an initial clinical trial, while separate CMTA-supported projects include a $201,000 CMT4B program and a $154,000 effort to engineer Schwann-cell-targeted AAV promoters. [CMTA CMT4 research portfolio](https://www.cmtausa.org/our-research/for-patients-and-families/cmta-star-research-for-demyelinating-cmt/cmt4/) Industry investment is also substantial: Novartis’ 2023 DTx Pharma transaction included a $500 million upfront payment and potential milestone payments, bringing a CMT1A-targeted RNA program and peripheral-tissue RNA-delivery platform into a major pharmaceutical company. [Novartis acquisition of DTx Pharma](https://www.novartis.com.cn/news/nuohuaxuanbushougou-dtx-pharmaqianghuashenjingkexuechanpinianhe-xrna-jishupingtai-nengli)

## Strengths, Limitations, and Challenges

The field’s major strength is that CMT is increasingly tractable as a collection of molecularly defined diseases rather than one disorder. CMT1A can potentially be treated by lowering PMP22; recessive forms such as CMT4C and CMT4J may be suitable for gene replacement; CMT-SORD has a measurable biochemical target; and dominant CMT2A may be addressed by selectively suppressing mutant activity while restoring normal protein. The preclinical studies are compelling because several have improved nerve structure and function, not merely symptoms. [AAV9 SH3TC2 gene replacement for CMT4C](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37641403/) [AAV9 FIG4 gene therapy for CMT4J](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33878035/) [SORD deficiency discovery](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41588-020-0615-4)

However, translating these advances remains difficult. CMT includes many genes and biological mechanisms, so one therapy will not cure every subtype; for dominant disorders, permanent gene editing must avoid damaging the healthy gene copy. Peripheral-nerve delivery, immune responses to viral vectors, dose control, durability, manufacturing cost, access, and the possibility that lost axons or muscle may not fully regenerate are major unresolved issues. Slow progression also makes trials long and statistically challenging, as shown by PXT3003’s negative Phase III primary result and ignaseclant’s failure to meet its short-term walking endpoint despite secondary signals. [PXT3003 PREMIER Phase III topline results](https://live.euronext.com/en/products/equities/company-news/2023-12-11-pharnext-reports-topline-results-pivotal-phase-iii) [SYNAPSE-CMT ignaseclant topline results](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/02/03/3230913/0/en/NMD-Pharma-announces-topline-results-from-its-Phase-2a-study-of-ignaseclant-in-Charcot-Marie-Tooth-disease-Types-1-and-2.html)

## Outlook and Future Directions

As of August 8, 2026, no therapy has demonstrated a clinical cure for any CMT subtype, but the outlook is more credible than it was in 2020 because treatment programs now span RNA silencing, gene replacement, gene editing, metabolic correction, cell therapy, and muscle-function enhancement. The most important milestones to watch are peer-reviewed and regulatory outcomes for govorestat in CMT-SORD; longer, controlled follow-up for ignaseclant; safety and efficacy data from EN001; and first-in-human translation of PMP22-lowering RNA therapies and AAV gene-replacement programs for recessive CMT. A true cure will likely arrive subtype by subtype and will depend on treating early enough to preserve peripheral nerves before irreversible degeneration occurs.
