# Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis

## Overview

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also called Lou Gehrig’s disease or motor neuron disease, is a progressive disorder in which nerve cells controlling voluntary movement die. It causes worsening weakness, muscle wasting, stiffness, difficulty speaking and swallowing, and eventually respiratory failure; thinking and behavior can also be affected in some people. ALS usually begins in adulthood, although some inherited forms start much earlier, and median survival is approximately three years from symptom onset, with substantial variation between individuals. [European ALS guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11235832/)

There is no cure for ALS. Current care combines an experienced multidisciplinary clinic, respiratory and nutrition support, physical and communication aids, symptom treatment, and advance-care planning. Riluzole is generally offered at diagnosis, edaravone may be considered for selected people, and the gene-targeted antisense drug tofersen is available in the United States for adults with ALS caused by a pathogenic *SOD1* variant; these treatments may slow aspects of disease but do not restore lost motor neurons. [European ALS guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11235832/) [FDA Qalsody approval](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/news-events-human-drugs/fda-approves-treatment-amyotrophic-lateral-sclerosis-associated-mutation-sod1-gene)

## Scope of Recent Research (2020–present)

ALS research has become more active and more biologically precise since 2020, with major efforts focused on silencing disease-causing genes, correcting toxic RNA and protein processes, protecting motor neurons and their supporting glial cells, and measuring whether a treatment is affecting disease before functional changes are detectable. The field is closest to meaningful disease modification in small genetic subgroups—especially *SOD1*-ALS—but no intervention has yet demonstrated that it can reliably stop, reverse, or prevent ALS across the broad population. [FDA Qalsody approval](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/news-events-human-drugs/fda-approves-treatment-amyotrophic-lateral-sclerosis-associated-mutation-sod1-gene) [BIIB078 C9orf72 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39059407/)

## Major Breakthroughs and Emerging Therapies

The most consequential advance has been RNA-targeted treatment for *SOD1*-ALS. Tofersen is an antisense oligonucleotide—an engineered short strand of nucleic acid that binds *SOD1* messenger RNA and reduces production of the SOD1 protein. In the randomized VALOR phase 3 study, it substantially lowered SOD1 protein in cerebrospinal fluid and neurofilament light chain (NfL), a blood marker of nerve injury, but did not significantly improve the primary clinical outcome at 28 weeks. In the subsequent open-label comparison, participants who started earlier had less functional decline at 52 weeks than those who started later, although that longer-term comparison was not randomized. [VALOR tofersen trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2204705) The FDA granted accelerated approval for tofersen, marketed as Qalsody, on April 25, 2023, based on the NfL reduction as a surrogate endpoint reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit. [FDA Qalsody approval](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/news-events-human-drugs/fda-approves-treatment-amyotrophic-lateral-sclerosis-associated-mutation-sod1-gene)

This success has strengthened the case for precision RNA medicines in other inherited forms of ALS. Jacifusen, also called ION363 or ulefnersen, is an antisense therapy designed to lower FUS protein in *FUS*-ALS, a particularly aggressive inherited form that often begins in young people. A 2025 case series reported molecular and clinical observations from treated individuals, while the randomized FUSION study continues to test efficacy, safety, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics in up to 95 participants. [Jacifusen case series](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40414240/) [FUSION trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04768972) By contrast, the phase 1 BIIB078 program targeting toxic *C9orf72* sense RNA did not reduce NfL or improve clinical outcomes relative to placebo, and development was discontinued, demonstrating that reaching the intended RNA target does not necessarily translate into clinical benefit. [BIIB078 C9orf72 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39059407/)

Gene therapy seeks a more durable version of gene silencing. AMT-162 is an investigational one-time intrathecal gene therapy using an adeno-associated virus serotype rh10 vector to deliver an artificial microRNA that suppresses *SOD1* expression; it is being evaluated in a phase 1/2 dose-escalation study in adults with *SOD1*-ALS. [AMT-162 trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06100276) Earlier first-in-human experience with an intrathecal AAV-miR-*SOD1* treatment in two people showed that delivery to the nervous system was possible, but also identified inflammatory toxicity requiring immunosuppression and was far too small to establish efficacy. [AAV-miR-SOD1 first-in-human report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32640133/)

Gene editing remains preclinical but could, in principle, permanently disable a toxic inherited gene rather than requiring repeated spinal injections. In *SOD1* mouse models, CRISPR/Cas9 editing prevented ALS-like disease in some experiments, while AAV-delivered base editing that introduced a stop signal into mutant *SOD1* slowed disease and prolonged survival. [CRISPR SOD1 mouse study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33767386/) [Base editing in ALS mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31991108/) These findings are important proof of concept, but no CRISPR-based ALS treatment has yet entered a human clinical trial.

Broader, non-gene-specific approaches remain necessary because most ALS is not caused by a single targetable mutation. The HEALEY ALS Platform Trial tests several candidate drugs through a shared infrastructure and shared placebo groups, allowing faster comparison of therapies. Its published pridopidine regimen did not slow ALS progression or improve survival-related outcomes over 24 weeks. [Pridopidine HEALEY trial](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2830509) Similarly, the larger phase 3 PHOENIX trial of sodium phenylbutyrate–taurursodiol, previously marketed as RELYVRIO/ALBRIOZA, failed its primary and secondary endpoints in 664 participants, underscoring the need for confirmatory trials even after encouraging earlier-phase findings. [PHOENIX phase 3 results](https://www.amylyx.com/news/amylyx-pharmaceuticals-announces-topline-results-from-global-phase-3-phoenix-trial-of-amx0035-in-als)

## Clinical Trials and Experimental Approaches

The key ALS precision-medicine trials are testing whether earlier intervention can preserve motor neurons before too much irreversible damage occurs. The ATLAS phase 3 trial is testing tofersen in genetically at-risk *SOD1* variant carriers who do not yet have clinical ALS symptoms, initiating treatment after a rise in plasma NfL and asking whether this delays clinically manifest disease or functional loss. [ATLAS trial design](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9587202/) Ionis Pharmaceuticals’ FUSION study is a phase 1–3 randomized trial of jacifusen in *FUS*-ALS, and uniQure’s AMT-162 trial is a phase 1/2, open-label, single-ascending-dose study of intrathecal AAV microRNA gene therapy in *SOD1*-ALS. [FUSION trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04768972) [AMT-162 trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06100276)

The HEALEY ALS Platform Trial, led through Massachusetts General Hospital and the Northeast ALS Consortium, is a phase 2/3 adaptive platform that can add or remove experimental regimens while using common procedures and shared control participants. [HEALEY platform trial commitment](https://www.als.org/stories-news/als-association-makes-multi-year-commitment-healey-als-platform-trial) Its pridopidine regimen enrolled 163 treated-or-regimen-placebo participants plus 122 shared-placebo participants and found no benefit on its primary function-and-survival outcome, providing a clear example of the platform’s ability to generate informative negative results efficiently. [Pridopidine HEALEY trial](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2830509)

Cell-based approaches remain experimental. NurOwn, an autologous mesenchymal stem-cell product intended to release neurotrophic factors and alter inflammation, did not meet its pivotal phase 3 efficacy endpoint; FDA reviewers identified uncertainty around the subgroup findings and the overall interpretation of the trial. [FDA NurOwn briefing document](https://www.fda.gov/media/172397/download) These results do not rule out a future role for cell-derived treatments, but they do not establish stem-cell transplantation as a cure or proven disease-modifying therapy for ALS.

## Methodologies and Scientific Approaches

Researchers increasingly divide ALS into biological subtypes using genetic testing, clinical phenotype, and molecular biomarkers. Blood NfL is especially important because it reflects ongoing axonal injury, predicts prognosis, and may support trial enrichment or stratified randomization; it also served as the surrogate endpoint supporting tofersen’s accelerated approval. [NfL prognostic-marker study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39270623/) [FDA Qalsody approval](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/news-events-human-drugs/fda-approves-treatment-amyotrophic-lateral-sclerosis-associated-mutation-sod1-gene) Trials commonly combine NfL with functional measures such as the ALS Functional Rating Scale–Revised, respiratory measures, survival, muscle strength, speech, and patient-reported outcomes. [VALOR tofersen trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2204705)

Preclinical research combines genetically engineered mice, patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), postmortem tissue, and increasingly complex human neuron and organoid models. These platforms allow investigators to study toxic SOD1, *C9orf72* repeat RNA and dipeptide-repeat proteins, and TDP-43 mislocalization—the abnormal movement of an RNA-binding protein from the nucleus into the cell body that is central to most ALS pathology. [Human TDP-43 iPSC model](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40704991/) Delivery technologies include repeated intrathecal antisense injections, AAV vectors carrying microRNAs, and experimental viral CRISPR or base-editing systems; each must demonstrate broad delivery to vulnerable motor neurons and supporting cells without unacceptable immune reactions or off-target genetic changes. [AAV-miR-SOD1 first-in-human report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32640133/) [CRISPR SOD1 mouse study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33767386/)

## Strengths, Limitations, and Challenges

The strongest recent evidence is that a therapy can engage a causal ALS target in people: tofersen lowers both SOD1 and NfL, and earlier initiation is biologically plausible and clinically promising for *SOD1*-ALS. [VALOR tofersen trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2204705) However, the pivotal randomized portion of VALOR did not meet its primary clinical endpoint, and Qalsody’s approval remains accelerated rather than full confirmation of clinical benefit. It also requires repeated lumbar punctures and carries risks including pain, fatigue, elevated cerebrospinal-fluid white cells, myalgia, and potentially serious neurologic adverse events. [FDA Qalsody approval](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/news-events-human-drugs/fda-approves-treatment-amyotrophic-lateral-sclerosis-associated-mutation-sod1-gene) [VALOR tofersen trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2204705)

The central challenge is biological heterogeneity. A treatment that lowers one toxic gene product may be highly relevant to a small inherited subgroup but not to the majority of people with sporadic ALS. The failure of BIIB078 in *C9orf72*-ALS and of PHOENIX in unselected ALS populations shows that encouraging mechanisms, biomarker effects, or earlier-stage signals can fail to produce meaningful clinical benefit. [BIIB078 C9orf72 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39059407/) [PHOENIX phase 3 results](https://www.amylyx.com/news/amylyx-pharmaceuticals-announces-topline-results-from-global-phase-3-phoenix-trial-of-amx0035-in-als) For gene therapy and editing, unresolved issues include delivering enough treatment throughout the brain and spinal cord, managing immune responses, avoiding irreversible off-target edits, and treating early enough to preserve motor neurons that have not already died. [CRISPR SOD1 mouse study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33767386/)

## Outlook and Future Directions

A cure for ALS is not imminent as of August 8, 2026, but the field has entered a more credible era of precision treatment development. The milestones to watch are whether presymptomatic tofersen in ATLAS delays *SOD1*-ALS, whether jacifusen produces convincing benefit in the FUSION trial, whether one-time *SOD1*-lowering gene therapy can be delivered safely and durably, and whether biomarker-guided platform trials can identify therapies for the much larger sporadic and TDP-43–related ALS populations. [ATLAS trial design](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9587202/) [FUSION trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04768972) [AMT-162 trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06100276) The realistic near-term goal is increasingly effective, earlier, subtype-specific disease modification; a broadly applicable cure will likely require combinations that address both the initiating cause and the downstream loss of motor neurons. [ALS Association research program](https://www.als.org/research)
