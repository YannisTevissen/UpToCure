# Ataxia-Telangiectasia

## Overview

Ataxia-telangiectasia (A-T) is a rare inherited condition caused by harmful changes in both copies of the *ATM* gene, which normally helps cells respond to DNA damage and oxidative stress. Classic A-T usually begins in early childhood with worsening problems in balance, coordination, speech, and eye movements; it can also cause immune deficiency, recurrent lung infections, marked sensitivity to ionizing radiation, and a high risk of cancer. Variant A-T can begin later and progress more slowly, but is still associated with neurologic disability and malignancy risk. [GeneReviews: Ataxia-Telangiectasia](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26468/)

Prognosis varies substantially by genetic subtype and complications, but classic A-T is life-limiting, with cancer, chronic lung disease, and infections major contributors to early mortality. There is no approved curative or disease-modifying treatment that restores *ATM* function throughout the body. Current care is multidisciplinary and supportive, including physical, occupational, speech, pulmonary, nutritional, and immunologic care; infection prevention and immunoglobulin replacement when indicated; cancer surveillance; and minimization of unnecessary ionizing-radiation exposure. [GeneReviews: Ataxia-Telangiectasia](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26468/)

## Scope of Recent Research (2020–present)

Since 2020, A-T research has become more therapeutically focused, particularly around restoring *ATM* activity in people with splice-altering mutations, protecting vulnerable neurons, correcting mitochondrial dysfunction, and improving measurable neurologic function in clinical trials. The field is active but remains early in curative development: the most direct molecular approach—mutation-specific antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs)—has reached individualized treatment and a small phase 1/2 study, whereas gene editing and broad gene-replacement concepts remain preclinical. [Individualized splice-switching ASO framework](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10371869/) [A-T clinical-trial landscape](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10530584/)

## Major Breakthroughs and Emerging Therapies

The most important advance toward addressing the root cause is splice-switching ASO therapy. ASOs are short synthetic strands of nucleic acid designed to bind RNA and alter how it is spliced before protein production. A 2023 study used whole-genome sequencing in 235 people with A-T to identify variants potentially amenable to this strategy; 9% were classified as probably amenable and 6% as possibly amenable. Researchers restored correct *ATM* RNA splicing and downstream ATM signaling in patient-derived fibroblasts for recurrent variants, then treated one child with the personalized ASO atipeksen for three years without serious adverse events. [Individualized splice-switching ASO framework](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10371869/)

Atipeksen is designed for the recurrent *ATM* c.7865C>T variant, which creates an abnormal splice site and prevents production of normal ATM protein. The drug is administered into cerebrospinal fluid by intrathecal injection and is intended to restore normal splicing of the affected RNA transcript. This is not yet a general A-T cure: it is mutation-specific, requires repeated dosing, and has not yet shown that it can reverse established neurologic disease or protect against all systemic complications of A-T. [A-T ASO phase 1/2 study](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07215416) [Individualized splice-switching ASO framework](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10371869/)

Gene editing is another potentially curative strategy, but it remains experimental. In 2020, researchers used CRISPR/Cas9-assisted correction in induced pluripotent stem cells—adult cells reprogrammed into a stem-cell-like state—from two people with A-T. Correcting the disease-causing *ATM* variants restored cellular responses to DNA damage and oxidative stress. This provides important proof of principle, but edited cells have not yet been safely delivered at scale to the brain, immune system, lungs, and other affected tissues in people with A-T. [CRISPR correction of ATM mutations in patient iPS cells](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32037450/)

Several non-curative approaches may nonetheless slow damage or improve function. Nicotinamide riboside, a vitamin B3-related NAD+ precursor, improved neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation, and motor performance in *Atm*-deficient mice, apparently by improving mitochondrial quality control. In a 24-person, four-month open-label study, nicotinamide riboside was associated with improved ataxia scores and higher immunoglobulin concentrations, but the absence of a placebo group means that efficacy remains uncertain. [NAD+ supplementation and mitophagy in A-T](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33734555/) [Nicotinamide riboside proof-of-concept trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34515380/)

Triheptanoin, a metabolic oil intended to replenish energy-producing pathways in mitochondria, produced encouraging phase 2a/b signals. In a 31-participant randomized, placebo-controlled dose-escalation study, the highest dose was associated with improvements in a nasal-cell mitochondrial stress outcome and selected motor, speech, and swallowing measures, although gastrointestinal adverse events led to dose capping at 20% of caloric intake in some participants. These findings support mitochondrial dysfunction as a modifiable feature of A-T, rather than demonstrating correction of the underlying genetic disorder. [Triheptanoin phase 2a/b trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40616902/)

Research is also identifying new targets downstream of ATM loss. A 2024 study found that ATM deficiency activated brain immune cells called microglia and linked this activation to neurodegeneration in A-T models and patient-derived material. This work supports testing approaches that reduce damaging neuroinflammation, but such approaches would likely be complementary to, rather than substitutes for, restoring ATM function. [ATM-deficiency-induced microglial activation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38159274/)

## Clinical Trials and Experimental Approaches

The most direct genetic trial is the Boston Children’s Hospital-sponsored phase 1/2 study of atipeksen, listed as recruiting as of August 8, 2026. The study plans to enroll 10 people with classic A-T who carry at least one copy of the *ATM* c.7865C>T variant. Its primary aim is safety; neurologic change will be measured with the A-T Neurological Examination Toolkit, structured global assessments, wearable movement measures, MRI, and biomarkers including neurofilament light chain and alpha-fetoprotein. No clinical efficacy results have yet been posted. [A-T ASO phase 1/2 study](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07215416)

A major 2026 clinical result came from IntraBio’s phase 3 trial of levacetylleucine, also called N-acetyl-L-leucine. In 73 participants aged four years or older, the randomized, double-blind crossover trial found a greater mean improvement in the Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia with levacetylleucine than with placebo, with no treatment-related serious adverse events or deaths. Although this is a meaningful symptomatic advance, levacetylleucine does not repair the *ATM* gene; its ongoing extension study is examining whether longer-term treatment has neuroprotective or disease-modifying effects. [Levacetylleucine phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42309084/)

Other advanced trials target neurologic decline rather than the causative mutation. Quince Therapeutics is conducting the phase 3 NEAT study of EryDex, in which dexamethasone sodium phosphate is encapsulated in a patient’s own red blood cells to reduce the systemic toxicity associated with conventional long-term steroid treatment. Its earlier phase 3 ATTeST study evaluated 175 participants, while the current NEAT study is designed as a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. [EryDex NEAT phase 3 study](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06193200) [EryDex ATTeST phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39152028/)

## Methodologies and Scientific Approaches

A-T researchers increasingly combine deep genetic diagnosis with patient-derived experimental systems. Whole-genome sequencing and RNA sequencing can reveal deep intronic or other splice-disrupting *ATM* variants missed by conventional exon-focused testing, identify people who could benefit from an ASO, and verify whether an ASO restores correct RNA processing and ATM signaling in fibroblasts before treatment. [Individualized splice-switching ASO framework](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10371869/)

The field is also improving disease models and outcome measures. Because many older *Atm*-deficient mouse strains did not reproduce the progressive ataxia seen in people, investigators have developed combined *Atm/Aptx* DNA-repair-deficient mouse models with progressive ataxia and cerebellar degeneration. These models are being studied with electrophysiology, calcium imaging, gene-expression analysis, brain-circuit recording, and Purkinje-neuron interventions. Clinical studies increasingly pair ataxia scales with MRI, digital wearables, neurofilament light chain, alpha-fetoprotein, cellular stress tests, and interferon-response signatures to detect biological effects in small populations. [NIH project: Defining cerebellar pathophysiology in A-T](https://taggs.hhs.gov/Detail/AwardDetail?arg_AwardNum=R01NS131646&arg_ProgOfficeCode=137) [Triheptanoin phase 2a/b trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40616902/)

## Leading Institutions and Funding

Boston Children’s Hospital, Harvard Medical School, the Broad Institute, and the A-T Children’s Project have been central to personalized ASO development, including atipeksen and the genomic framework for identifying splice-correctable *ATM* variants. The University of Queensland and Queensland Children’s Hospital have led mitochondrial and metabolic studies of nicotinamide riboside and triheptanoin. IntraBio has sponsored the global levacetylleucine phase 3 program, while Quince Therapeutics is sponsoring EryDex’s current pivotal development program. ## Strengths, Limitations, and Challenges

A major strength of current A-T research is that several approaches are now being tested in people: symptom-directed drugs have reached phase 3, metabolic therapies have generated controlled phase 2 data, and personalized RNA therapy has moved from patient cells to intrathecal clinical treatment. The ASO strategy is especially compelling because it can restore the patient’s own full-length *ATM* transcript for particular splice variants, rather than merely treating a consequence of the disease. [Individualized splice-switching ASO framework](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10371869/) [Levacetylleucine phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42309084/)

The central limitation is that A-T is a whole-body DNA-repair disorder, not solely a cerebellar disorder. A durable cure would need to safely restore adequate ATM activity in the central nervous system, immune cells, lung-relevant tissues, and cancer-prone cells, ideally before irreversible neuron loss occurs. ASOs currently apply only to subsets of mutations and require delivery across or around the blood-brain barrier; gene editing faces major delivery, off-target, DNA-damage, and long-term cancer-safety concerns in a disorder already defined by genome instability. Hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation may help selected immune or cancer-related complications, but published experience remains limited and inconsistent regarding neurologic benefit. [A-T ASO phase 1/2 study](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07215416) [Hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42051534/) [DFG: Hematopoietic stem-cell gene therapy for A-T](https://gepris.dfg.de/gepris/projekt/428639696?language=en)

## Outlook and Future Directions

As of August 8, 2026, A-T is not close to a universal cure, but the field has moved beyond purely supportive care. The most consequential milestones to watch are clinical safety and neurologic results from atipeksen; expansion of ASO programs to additional *ATM* splice variants; validation of biomarkers that can show target engagement and slower neurodegeneration; longer-term outcomes from levacetylleucine, EryDex, and metabolic interventions; and preclinical demonstrations that gene editing or gene delivery can safely restore ATM across the brain and peripheral tissues. A realistic near-term goal is mutation-specific disease modification and earlier treatment, while a broadly applicable cure will require solving systemic delivery and long-term safety.
