# CHARGE Syndrome

## Overview

CHARGE syndrome is a rare, usually sporadic genetic condition most often caused by a harmful change in one copy of **CHD7**, a gene that helps control when many other genes are switched on during early development. The name refers to common features—coloboma (a gap in eye structures), heart defects, choanal atresia or stenosis (blocked or narrowed nasal passages), growth and developmental differences, genital differences, and ear abnormalities including hearing loss—but individuals can also have feeding and breathing problems, balance and cranial-nerve abnormalities, kidney differences, and deafblindness. [CHD7 Disorder](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1117/)

Prognosis varies greatly with the severity of congenital heart, airway, feeding, respiratory, and neurologic complications. Mortality is highest in early childhood for people with severe defects, while many affected people can live into adulthood with normal life expectancy. There is currently no therapy that corrects the underlying CHD7-related developmental disorder; standard care is individualized, lifelong multidisciplinary management, including surgery when needed, feeding and respiratory support, hearing/vision services, endocrine care, developmental therapies, and ongoing surveillance. [CHD7 Disorder](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1117/)

## Scope of Recent Research (2020–present)

Research since 2020 has been scientifically active but remains overwhelmingly preclinical: investigators are defining how reduced CHD7 function disrupts chromatin—the packaging system that helps regulate gene activity—during formation of the brain, inner ear, heart, face, and other organs. The main therapeutic questions are whether raising output from the remaining working CHD7 copy, correcting downstream signaling defects, or intervening early enough to protect specific functions can help. A 2025 analysis found that nearly all identified U.S. NIH CHARGE-related funding through 2024 was basic (T0) research rather than human translation, so a curative therapy is not close at present. [NIH funding analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12884510/)

## Major Breakthroughs and Emerging Therapies

**Small molecules and pathway correction.** The strongest recent therapeutic-style preclinical result is a 2021 study in CHD7-deficient zebrafish and *Caenorhabditis elegans*. The researchers found reduced development of GABA-producing inhibitory neurons, abnormal MAPK/ERK signaling, and hyperactivity-like behavior in the zebrafish model; the small molecule ephedrine normalized MAPK/ERK signaling and improved both neuronal and behavioral abnormalities in those models. This is an important proof of principle for treating a downstream consequence of CHD7 loss, not evidence that ephedrine is safe, effective, or appropriate for people with CHARGE syndrome. [Ephedrine screen in CHD7 models](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8183419/)

**RNA-based dosage restoration.** CHARGE syndrome is commonly driven by *haploinsufficiency*, meaning one working CHD7 copy does not make enough functional protein. In 2024, the CHARGE Syndrome Foundation funded “SINEUP for CHARGE,” led by Albert Basson at the University of Exeter and Stefano Espinoza at the University of Eastern Piedmont. SINEUPs are antisense long non-coding RNAs designed to increase translation of a chosen messenger RNA into protein, offering a plausible way to boost production from an intact CHD7 allele without changing DNA. This remains an early research project; no CHD7-targeted SINEUP clinical results have been reported. [Foundation grant archive](https://www.chargesyndrome.org/overview/research/foundation-supported-research/foundation-research-grant-recipients-archive/) [SINEUP design and delivery](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK584241/)

**Epigenetic and developmental mechanism research.** A 2021 mouse study showed that CHD7 is needed to keep important gene-control regions, called enhancers, active in developing cerebellar granule-cell precursors. Removing CHD7 changed enhancer activity and expression of many neurodevelopmental genes and caused abnormal cerebellar folding in mice. These findings do not yet identify a medicine, but they sharpen the search for druggable downstream pathways and define developmental windows in which interventions may have to act. [CHD7 enhancer regulation in cerebellum](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8481233/)

**Gene therapy and gene editing.** No CHARGE-specific gene-replacement, genome-editing, cell-therapy, or RNA-therapy clinical program was identified in the current clinical-trial record or in the recent NIH funding landscape. A central obstacle is that CHD7 acts across many tissues during embryonic and early postnatal development, so delivering a precisely dosed therapy to the right cells at the right time—and reversing malformations already present at birth—will be far more difficult than treating a single-organ disease. [NIH funding analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12884510/) [CHD7 Disorder](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1117/)

## Clinical Trials and Experimental Approaches

As of August 8, 2026, no registered interventional clinical trial testing a disease-modifying or curative CHARGE syndrome therapy was identified. The notable CHARGE entry on ClinicalTrials.gov, sponsored by Poitiers University Hospital, was a completed diagnostic and molecular cohort study of 141 participants; it used blood collection for genetic analysis, had no therapeutic phase, and completed in December 2015. It therefore does not provide treatment outcomes. [French clinical and molecular CHARGE study](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03186144?cond=CHARGE+Syndrome)

The current experimental pipeline instead consists of laboratory and model-system work. Examples include CHARGE patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), which can be turned into disease-relevant cell types, and human inner-ear organoids carrying CHD7 variants, which are being used to test how CHD7 defects alter hair-cell development and whether secreted factors can rescue abnormal otic differentiation. [CHARGE patient iPSC line](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1873506120300787) [Human inner-ear organoids project](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/projects/modeling-charge-syndrome-with-human-inner-ear-organoids/)

## Methodologies and Scientific Approaches

Researchers combine genetically engineered zebrafish, mice, worms, frogs, human iPSCs, and three-dimensional organoids to connect specific CHD7 variants with cellular defects. Zebrafish models are particularly useful for observing craniofacial and cardiovascular development and for rapid drug screening; a 2022 study developed *chd7* mutant fish with craniofacial cartilage and aortic-arch abnormalities resembling aspects of CHARGE syndrome. [CHD7 zebrafish craniofacial and cardiac model](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cell-and-developmental-biology/articles/10.3389/fcell.2022.1030587/full)

Modern platforms include single-cell RNA sequencing, chromatin-accessibility profiling, CHD7-binding assays, proteomics, lineage tracing, and long-read genome and methylation sequencing. These methods aim to identify biomarkers, distinguish effects of different CHD7 variants, uncover modifier pathways, and create measurable laboratory outcomes for future therapeutic screens. [Human inner-ear organoids project](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/projects/modeling-charge-syndrome-with-human-inner-ear-organoids/) [Foundation grant archive](https://www.chargesyndrome.org/overview/research/foundation-supported-research/foundation-research-grant-recipients-archive/)

## Leading Institutions and Funding

Important research groups include the University of Michigan, University of Southern California and Children’s Hospital Los Angeles, University of Exeter, University of Kentucky, SickKids Research Institute, Radboud University, the German Cancer Research Center, and institutions in France, Italy, Canada, and the Netherlands. The CHARGE Syndrome Foundation has supported projects ranging from SINEUP RNA research and epigenetic normalization studies to optic-fissure, hearing, sleep, and motor-intervention research; its individual grants are available for up to $50,000, and it reports that prior grantees have subsequently obtained nearly $12 million from NIH and other government agencies. [Foundation-supported research](https://www.chargesyndrome.org/overview/research/foundation-supported-research/) [Foundation grant archive](https://www.chargesyndrome.org/overview/research/foundation-supported-research/foundation-research-grant-recipients-archive/)

NIH support is substantial in aggregate but has not yet produced a translational clinical pipeline. The recent funding analysis identified $64.3 million in NIH CHARGE-related funding across 45 projects from 2000 through 2024, with essentially all funding classified as basic research. A newer NICHD R21 award, “CHD7-dependent regulation of migration and morphogenesis,” runs from June 1, 2026, through May 31, 2028 and uses worm and frog models to study extracellular-matrix and developmental mechanisms. [NIH funding analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12884510/) [NICHD CHD7 migration award](https://taggs.hhs.gov/Detail/AwardDetail?arg_AwardNum=R21HD122393&arg_ProgOfficeCode=50)

## Strengths, Limitations, and Challenges

The field’s main strength is its increasingly precise understanding that reduced CHD7 dosage disrupts gene regulation in multiple developmental cell types, along with a growing set of animal, stem-cell, and organoid models for testing hypotheses. The ephedrine study demonstrates that at least some CHD7-linked cellular and behavioral abnormalities can be improved after the genetic defect is present in animal models, and dosage-enhancing RNA strategies are conceptually well matched to haploinsufficiency. [Ephedrine screen in CHD7 models](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8183419/) [SINEUP design and delivery](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK584241/)

The limitations are formidable. CHARGE syndrome affects many organs before birth, CHD7 has different functions in different tissues and developmental stages, and the syndrome’s severity and manifestations differ substantially between individuals. A systemic treatment may need exceptionally careful dose control, broad but safe delivery, and early timing; it may also be unable to reverse established structural defects such as major heart, airway, eye, or inner-ear malformations. The absence of therapeutic clinical trials and the concentration of funding in basic research remain major barriers to establishing safety, meaningful outcome measures, access, and eventual regulatory approval. [CHD7 Disorder](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1117/) [NIH funding analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12884510/)

## Outlook and Future Directions

A whole-body cure for CHARGE syndrome is not imminent. The most credible nearer-term milestones are confirmation of drug-responsive pathways in patient-derived cells and animal models; publication of results from CHD7 dosage-restoration approaches such as SINEUPs; validation of inner-ear, brain, and neural-crest organoid biomarkers; and development of natural-history studies and trial-ready clinical outcome measures. If these steps succeed, the first disease-modifying interventions are more likely to target particular complications or developmental pathways than to reverse every feature of CHARGE syndrome.
