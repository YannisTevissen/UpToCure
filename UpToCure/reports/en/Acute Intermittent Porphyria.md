# Acute Intermittent Porphyria

## Overview

Acute intermittent porphyria (AIP) is an inherited disorder of heme production, caused by a harmful change in one copy of the *HMBS* gene. When liver demand for heme rises, the partially blocked pathway can overproduce aminolevulinic acid (ALA) and porphobilinogen (PBG), chemicals associated with severe “neurovisceral” attacks that can include abdominal pain, vomiting, fast heart rate, high blood pressure, weakness, and psychiatric or neurologic symptoms. Most people who carry an *HMBS* variant never develop symptomatic disease, but attacks can be disabling or life-threatening in those who do. [GeneReviews: Acute Intermittent Porphyria](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1193/?report=reader) [ENVISION phase 3 trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1913147)

Prognosis is generally good when AIP is recognized promptly and attacks are treated before major nerve injury occurs, although some people develop chronic pain, kidney disease, hypertension, or liver complications. Standard care includes avoiding known triggers, promptly treating attacks with intravenous hemin, and using the monthly small-interfering RNA medicine givosiran to prevent recurrent attacks in appropriate patients. A liver transplant can stop the liver’s overproduction of toxic precursors and is the only established disease-correcting intervention, but it is reserved for exceptionally severe disease because it requires a donor organ and lifelong immunosuppression. [American Porphyria Foundation: AIP](https://porphyriafoundation.org/for-patients/types-of-porphyria/aip/) [GeneReviews: Acute Intermittent Porphyria](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1193/?report=reader)

## Scope of Recent Research (2020–present)

Research since 2020 has been active but concentrated in a small rare-disease field. The central question is whether AIP can be controlled by lowering toxic precursor production, as givosiran does, or corrected at its source by restoring hepatic HMBS/PBGD enzyme activity through gene replacement, messenger RNA (mRNA) replacement, or mutation-sensitive protein stabilization. The field has produced major advances in disease control and strong animal evidence for etiologic treatment, but, as of August 8, 2026, no gene replacement, gene-editing, or cell-based therapy has demonstrated a durable clinical cure in people with AIP. [Systemic hPBGD mRNA replacement in primates](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39366725/) [Therapeutic strategies for AIP](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33139979/)

## Major Breakthroughs and Emerging Therapies

**RNA interference: highly effective disease suppression, not gene correction.** Givosiran is a small-interfering RNA treatment that reduces liver production of ALAS1, the first regulated enzyme in the heme-production pathway. In the pivotal phase 3 ENVISION trial, participants with AIP receiving givosiran had a mean annualized attack rate of 3.2 compared with 12.5 with placebo, a 74% reduction. This was a landmark because it showed that sustained lowering of ALA and PBG can sharply reduce attacks and hemin use; however, givosiran does not repair the inherited *HMBS* variant and must be administered repeatedly. [ENVISION phase 3 trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1913147)

**mRNA enzyme replacement: the leading etiologic experimental approach.** Instead of suppressing the pathway upstream, lipid nanoparticles can carry mRNA encoding normal human PBGD, also called HMBS, into liver cells. The cells then temporarily make the missing enzyme. In a 2025 study, researchers created a clinically relevant non-human-primate AIP model by selectively reducing liver PBGD, then provoking attacks with porphyrinogenic drugs. Repeated systemic doses of human PBGD mRNA restored liver PBGD activity and protected against biochemical attacks, metabolic abnormalities, and central-nervous-system changes in the animals. This is especially important because it tests an approach that addresses the enzyme deficiency itself, although it remains preclinical and would still require repeat dosing. [Systemic hPBGD mRNA replacement in primates](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39366725/)

**Gene replacement: biologically compelling, but not yet clinically adequate.** A single-dose adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector could, in principle, deliver a functional *HMBS* gene to hepatocytes and provide long-term enzyme production. The first human AAV2/5-PBGD study established that intravenous treatment was generally tolerated in eight people with severe AIP, but urinary ALA and PBG did not normalize at the tested doses. Although hospitalizations and hemin treatment trended downward, the trial did not achieve metabolic correction, emphasizing that sufficient liver delivery and durable expression remain the key barriers. [Phase I liver-directed PBGD gene therapy](https://www.journal-of-hepatology.eu/article/S0168-8278%2816%2930198-2/fulltext)

**Pharmacological chaperones: mutation-selective small molecules.** A separate preclinical strategy seeks to stabilize unstable mutant HMBS proteins rather than replace the gene or its product. A 2020 study identified a small-molecule pharmacological chaperone that increased stability and cellular abundance of certain HMBS variants and improved enzyme activity in experimental systems. This strategy could eventually be useful for people whose specific mutations produce a partly functional but unstable protein, but it is unlikely to work across all *HMBS* variants and has not yet entered human efficacy trials. [Pharmacological chaperone therapy for AIP](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31810863/)

## Clinical Trials and Experimental Approaches

The major recent clinical evidence remains givosiran rather than a cure-directed product. In ENVISION, a randomized phase 3 trial sponsored by Alnylam Pharmaceuticals, givosiran substantially reduced attack frequency in AIP; the ClinicalTrials.gov record describes a 94-person acute hepatic porphyria study with a six-month blinded period followed by an open-label extension. [ENVISION phase 3 trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1913147) [NCT03338816: ENVISION](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03338816?rank=1&term=NCT03338816)

Longer-term follow-up is encouraging for disease control but does not establish cure. A completed phase 1/2 open-label extension, sponsored by Alnylam and enrolling 16 people with AIP, followed participants for up to 48 months and reported sustained clinical improvements with an acceptable safety profile. Givosiran’s U.S. prescribing information nevertheless requires monitoring for risks including injection-site reactions, nausea, renal-function decline, liver-enzyme elevations, hypersensitivity, and pancreatitis. [NCT02949830: long-term givosiran study](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02949830) [48-month givosiran follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39363243/) [FDA GIVLAARI prescribing information](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2024/212194s007lbl.pdf)

The prior phase I AAV2/5-PBGD gene-therapy study remains the only completed human gene-replacement trial specifically designed for AIP. Current registered AIP studies include research into genetic modifiers of why some *HMBS* carriers become symptomatic and others do not, as well as a recruiting study of carbohydrate-rich dietary treatment; neither is a curative gene-therapy trial. [NCT02082860: AAV2/5-PBGD gene therapy](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02082860?cond=%22Porphyria%2C+Erythropoietic%22&rank=1&term=gene+therapy&viewType=Table) [NCT05502133: AIP modifying genes](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05502133) [NCT06273644: carbohydrate-rich diet study](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06273644)

## Methodologies and Scientific Approaches

Researchers measure urinary or plasma ALA and PBG as core biochemical indicators of pathway overactivity, alongside attack frequency, hemin use, hospitalizations, pain and neurologic measures, kidney and liver function, and quality of life. Genetic confirmation of an *HMBS* variant helps establish inherited susceptibility, while elevated urinary PBG during symptoms helps confirm that an acute attack is porphyric rather than caused by another condition. [GeneReviews: Acute Intermittent Porphyria](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1193/?report=reader)

The most advanced experimental platforms are liver-directed. AAV vectors are engineered to introduce an *HMBS* DNA expression cassette into hepatocytes, whereas lipid nanoparticles deliver short-lived HMBS mRNA without changing genomic DNA. Recent primate work used liver-targeted RNA interference to create an AIP-like deficiency, porphyrinogenic drugs to induce attacks, and multimodal assessments including liver metabolism, brain perfusion, cerebral glucose uptake, motor behavior, and biochemical precursors to test whether mRNA replacement reverses disease features. [Systemic hPBGD mRNA replacement in primates](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39366725/)

## Leading Institutions and Funding

The University of Navarra’s Center for Applied Medical Research (CIMA), Clínica Universidad de Navarra, and European collaborators have been central to the AAV-PBGD and mRNA-replacement programs. The earlier European Commission FP7 AIPGENE project supported development of liver-directed PBGD gene therapy; reported EU contributions included €1.09 million to Amsterdam Molecular Therapeutics and €508,800 to the University of Navarra. ## Strengths, Limitations, and Challenges

The field’s chief strength is a clear liver-centered disease mechanism: lowering ALAS1 or restoring PBGD can reduce the buildup of ALA and PBG. Givosiran has validated ALA/PBG reduction as a clinically meaningful strategy, while primate mRNA data provide unusually relevant preclinical support for restoring the missing enzyme. Liver transplantation also demonstrates that correcting the hepatic source can eliminate acute attacks. [ENVISION phase 3 trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1913147) [Systemic hPBGD mRNA replacement in primates](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39366725/) [American Porphyria Foundation: AIP](https://porphyriafoundation.org/for-patients/types-of-porphyria/aip/)

The central limitation is translation from models to a durable, safe, accessible treatment in people. AAV gene therapy was safe at early tested doses but failed to normalize precursors, and anti-AAV antibodies can limit eligibility for first treatment or later redosing. mRNA replacement avoids permanent viral-vector exposure and does not edit DNA, but its temporary enzyme expression means that dose frequency, lipid-nanoparticle safety, immune effects, manufacturing, and cost must be established clinically. Finally, neither successful prevention of attacks nor normalization of precursor levels automatically proves reversal of chronic pain, neuropathy, kidney disease, or liver-cancer risk. [Phase I liver-directed PBGD gene therapy](https://www.journal-of-hepatology.eu/article/S0168-8278%2816%2930198-2/fulltext) [Systemic hPBGD mRNA replacement in primates](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39366725/) [FDA GIVLAARI prescribing information](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2024/212194s007lbl.pdf)

## Outlook and Future Directions

A practical, non-transplant cure for AIP is not imminent, but the path is clearer than it was in 2020. The milestones to watch are initiation of a first-in-human HMBS/PBGD mRNA-replacement trial, demonstration that repeated dosing safely normalizes ALA and PBG and prevents attacks in people, and a next-generation liver gene-transfer program that achieves sufficient expression without unacceptable immune barriers. Until then, givosiran represents major progress in preventing attacks, whereas liver transplantation remains the only established intervention that corrects the liver-based biochemical source of severe AIP.
