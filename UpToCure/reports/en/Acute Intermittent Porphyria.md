# Acute Intermittent Porphyria

## Overview

Acute Intermittent Porphyria (AIP) is a rare inherited disorder of heme production caused by reduced activity of hydroxymethylbilane synthase, also called HMBS or porphobilinogen deaminase; when the liver increases heme-pathway activity, toxic precursors called aminolevulinic acid (ALA) and porphobilinogen (PBG) can accumulate and cause attacks of severe abdominal pain, nausea, constipation or diarrhea, high blood pressure, fast heart rate, psychiatric symptoms, seizures, and nerve injury [GeneReviews AIP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1193/). AIP has low penetrance, meaning many people with an HMBS pathogenic variant never develop attacks, but symptomatic disease can be disabling and attacks may require hospitalization, prolonged recovery, and rarely can be fatal [GeneReviews AIP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1193/).

The current standard of care is not curative and focuses on avoiding triggers, diagnosing attacks by elevated urinary PBG, using carbohydrate or intravenous glucose for mild attacks, giving intravenous hemin for moderate or severe attacks, treating pain and nausea, correcting electrolyte problems, and monitoring long-term complications [GeneReviews AIP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1193/). For people with recurrent attacks, givosiran is an approved preventive small interfering RNA therapy that reduces liver ALAS1 expression and is indicated for adults with acute hepatic porphyria in the United States and for adults and adolescents aged 12 years and older in the European Union [FDA Givlaari label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2019/021219s000lbl.pdf), [EMA Givlaari overview](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/human/EPAR/givlaari).

## Scope of Recent Research (2020–present)

From 2020 to May 2026, AIP research has been active but concentrated in a small number of liver-directed strategies: RNA interference to suppress the overactive heme pathway, messenger RNA replacement to transiently supply HMBS, and gene replacement to restore HMBS more durably [NEJM givosiran phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32640133/), [first-in-human HMBS mRNA trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9378398/), [gene therapy review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10744145/). The field has made real progress in preventing attacks, but recent reviews still describe treatment options as limited and curative therapy as an unmet goal rather than a near-term clinical reality [2025 AIP therapy overview](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40046218/).

## Major Breakthroughs and Emerging Therapies

The major recent clinical breakthrough is givosiran, a monthly RNA interference drug that silences ALAS1, the first liver enzyme in the heme pathway, thereby lowering production of the neurotoxic precursors ALA and PBG [NEJM givosiran phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32640133/). In the phase 3 ENVISION trial, 94 patients with AIP and recurrent attacks were randomized to givosiran or placebo, and givosiran reduced the mean annualized composite attack rate from 12.5 to 3.2 attacks, a 74% lower rate, while also reducing hemin-use days and urinary ALA and PBG [NEJM givosiran phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32640133/). This is disease-modifying rather than curative because it suppresses an upstream enzyme but does not correct the HMBS defect [gene therapy review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10744145/).

Gene replacement is the most direct curative concept because AIP is caused by HMBS haploinsufficiency and because liver-directed correction is expected to address the main source of excess ALA and PBG [gene therapy review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10744145/). The AAV2/5-hPBGD program tested an adeno-associated viral vector carrying the human PBGD/HMBS gene under a liver-specific promoter in an open-label phase 1 dose-ranging trial of eight patients [AAV2/5-hPBGD trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02082860). Recent gene-therapy reviews emphasize that this approach established feasibility but also highlighted barriers including vector dose, durability of expression, immune responses, manufacturing complexity, and pre-existing anti-AAV immunity [gene therapy review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10744145/).

Messenger RNA therapy is a second replacement approach in which lipid nanoparticles deliver HMBS mRNA so liver cells temporarily make the missing enzyme [first-in-human HMBS mRNA trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9378398/). In preclinical AIP models, intravenous lipid-nanoparticle HMBS mRNA produced liver HMBS protein, increased HMBS activity, and reduced urinary ALA and PBG, with an optimized mRNA construct showing stronger expression and improved biochemical effects [preclinical HMBS mRNA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35487293/). However, in a first-in-human phase 1/2 trial of 16 patients, HMBS mRNA was generally tolerated but did not significantly reduce urinary ALA or PBG, and the study was stopped early because robust benefit was unlikely at the tested regimen [first-in-human HMBS mRNA trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9378398/).

Small-molecule and metabolic approaches remain important supportive tools but have not become cures. Glucose and hemin work by reducing hepatic ALAS1 induction during attacks, while prevention still depends on avoiding porphyrogenic drugs, fasting, alcohol excess, infections, and other triggers [GeneReviews AIP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1193/). Recent therapy reviews describe continued interest in better pathway control and safer long-term management, but the most cure-oriented strategies remain HMBS replacement rather than conventional small molecules [2025 AIP therapy overview](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40046218/).

Cell or organ replacement provides proof of biology but is not a practical broad cure. Orthotopic liver transplantation can correct severe refractory hepatic AIP manifestations, supporting the idea that restoring liver HMBS activity can prevent attacks, but transplantation carries major operative risk, donor-organ limits, and lifelong immunosuppression [first-in-human HMBS mRNA trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9378398/).

## Clinical Trials and Experimental Approaches

The pivotal recent trial is ENVISION, a phase 3 randomized placebo-controlled study sponsored by Alnylam that tested monthly subcutaneous givosiran in patients with AIP who had at least two attacks in the previous six months [NEJM givosiran phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32640133/). Reported outcomes included a 74% lower mean annualized attack rate, a 77% lower mean annualized number of hemin-use days, lower urinary ALA and PBG, and more injection-site reactions and nausea in the givosiran group than in the placebo group [NEJM givosiran phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32640133/). A phase 1/2 open-label extension study of givosiran in AIP enrolled 16 patients, evaluated long-term safety and attack-rate outcomes, and lists actual study completion in December 2024 [givosiran extension trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03338816).

The main gene-transfer clinical experiment is AAV2/5-hPBGD, a multicenter, open-label, single-dose phase 1 trial designed to assess safety and maximum tolerated dose of a liver-directed recombinant adeno-associated viral vector carrying the human PBGD/HMBS gene [AAV2/5-hPBGD trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02082860). Although this trial started before 2020, it remains central to post-2020 cure research because recent reviews use it to define what next-generation AAV programs must improve: stronger liver expression, safer dosing, and durable biochemical correction [gene therapy review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10744145/).

The first-in-human HMBS mRNA phase 1/2 trial tested intravenous lipid-nanoparticle mRNA in patients with intermittent acute attacks and patients receiving scheduled hemin [first-in-human HMBS mRNA trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9378398/). The trial reported no treatment-related serious or severe adverse events, but transient liver transaminase elevations occurred after multiple doses in six of seven subjects, no significant ALA or PBG reductions were observed, and development was stopped early for lack of expected efficacy [first-in-human HMBS mRNA trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9378398/).

## Methodologies and Scientific Approaches

Researchers investigate AIP cures using genetic diagnosis, biochemical biomarkers, liver-focused pharmacology, and animal models. Urinary ALA and PBG are central biomarkers because they rise during attacks and fall when the pathogenic liver pathway is successfully suppressed or corrected [GeneReviews AIP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1193/), [NEJM givosiran phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32640133/). Preclinical mRNA studies use genetic AIP mouse models and chemically induced attacks to test whether HMBS replacement lowers ALA, PBG, ALAS1 induction, and inflammatory markers [preclinical HMBS mRNA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35487293/).

The dominant platforms are liver-directed nucleic-acid delivery systems. Givosiran uses small interfering RNA to reduce ALAS1 expression in hepatocytes [FDA Givlaari label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2019/021219s000lbl.pdf), mRNA therapy uses lipid nanoparticles to transiently express HMBS [first-in-human HMBS mRNA trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9378398/), and gene therapy programs use recombinant adeno-associated viral vectors to deliver HMBS/PBGD DNA to the liver [AAV2/5-hPBGD trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02082860). Across platforms, key readouts include attack frequency, hemin use, quality of life, urinary ALA and PBG, liver enzymes, kidney function, and longer-term tolerability [NEJM givosiran phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32640133/).

## Leading Institutions and Funding

Alnylam Pharmaceuticals has led the most successful recent AIP drug-development program through givosiran, which is approved by the FDA and EMA for acute hepatic porphyria populations [FDA Givlaari label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2019/021219s000lbl.pdf), [EMA Givlaari overview](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/human/EPAR/givlaari). International porphyria centers contributed to the ENVISION trial, and academic-clinical investigators led or participated in the HMBS mRNA study published in Molecular Therapy [NEJM givosiran phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32640133/), [first-in-human HMBS mRNA trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9378398/).

The AAV2/5-hPBGD trial record identifies CIMA, University of Navarra, the UNITE porfiria Foundation, and Laboratorios VIVEbiotech as collaborators, illustrating the academic-foundation-biotechnology partnerships that are typical in rare-disease cure research [AAV2/5-hPBGD trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02082860). Public infrastructure from the U.S. National Library of Medicine also supports the field by maintaining GeneReviews clinical resources and ClinicalTrials.gov trial records used by clinicians, patients, and researchers [GeneReviews AIP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1193/), [givosiran extension trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03338816).

## Strengths, Limitations, and Challenges

AIP has several strengths as a cure target: the causative gene is known, the liver is the key therapeutic organ for attacks, and ALA and PBG provide measurable biomarkers of biochemical response [GeneReviews AIP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1193/), [gene therapy review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10744145/). Givosiran has shown that targeted liver RNA therapy can substantially reduce attacks and hemin use, but it requires ongoing treatment and does not replace HMBS [NEJM givosiran phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32640133/), [FDA Givlaari label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2019/021219s000lbl.pdf).

The main challenge for a true cure is achieving enough safe, durable HMBS restoration in the liver. AAV gene therapy must overcome immune responses, pre-existing anti-vector antibodies, dose-related toxicity, and uncertainty about long-term expression [gene therapy review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10744145/). mRNA therapy avoids permanent DNA delivery and can be redosed in principle, but the first clinical HMBS mRNA trial did not produce significant biochemical improvement and showed transient liver enzyme elevations after repeated dosing [first-in-human HMBS mRNA trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9378398/). Access is also a practical limitation because rare-disease RNA and gene therapies require specialist monitoring and may be difficult to deliver equitably across health systems [EMA Givlaari overview](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/human/EPAR/givlaari).

## Outlook and Future Directions

As of May 2026, AIP has an effective preventive therapy for many patients with recurrent attacks, but it does not yet have a widely available cure [NEJM givosiran phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32640133/), [2025 AIP therapy overview](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40046218/). The milestones to watch are long-term real-world safety and durability data for givosiran, improved patient selection for preventive therapy, renewed HMBS replacement programs with better mRNA or nonviral delivery, and next-generation AAV or other gene-replacement approaches that can show sustained ALA/PBG normalization, fewer attacks, and acceptable liver and immune safety [givosiran extension trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03338816), [preclinical HMBS mRNA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35487293/), [gene therapy review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10744145/).
