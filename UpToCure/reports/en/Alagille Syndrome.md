# Alagille Syndrome

## Overview

Alagille syndrome (ALGS) is a rare, usually autosomal-dominant genetic disorder caused by a disease-causing variant in *JAG1* or, less often, *NOTCH2*. These genes help control Notch signaling, a developmental communication system that is important for forming bile ducts and several other organs. ALGS can therefore affect the liver, heart and blood vessels, kidneys, eyes, skeleton, growth, and development; severity varies greatly, including among relatives with the same genetic variant. [GeneReviews overview](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1273/)

The most serious and common problem is reduced numbers of small bile ducts in the liver, which can cause cholestasis (impaired bile flow), jaundice, poor growth, vitamin deficiencies, fatty skin deposits, and severe itching. Prognosis ranges from mild disease to life-threatening liver or cardiac complications; some people require liver transplantation for refractory cholestasis or end-stage liver disease. Current care is multidisciplinary and includes nutritional and fat-soluble-vitamin support, treatment of itching and cholestasis, cardiac and other organ-specific care, surgery in selected cases, and transplantation when needed. The approved ileal bile acid transporter inhibitors maralixibat and odevixibat increase bile-acid excretion and improve symptoms, but they do not correct the underlying genetic disorder. [GeneReviews management](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1273/)

## Scope of Recent Research (2020–present)

The ALGS field has become more active since 2020, but cure-directed research remains small and largely preclinical. The main questions are whether restoring *JAG1*/Notch activity after birth can rebuild the intrahepatic bile-duct network, which cells must be targeted, and whether treatment can safely help a multisystem developmental disorder without excessive Notch signaling. Recent work has produced credible proof-of-concept strategies in animal and cell models, but no gene therapy, RNA therapy, gene-editing treatment, or cell-replacement therapy has entered human clinical testing for ALGS. [JAG1-upregulating ASO study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12446204/) [AAV-SOX4 mouse study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40446945/)

## Major Breakthroughs and Emerging Therapies

**RNA therapeutics to raise JAG1 protein.** A 2025 mouse study tested antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), short modified nucleic-acid medicines that bind RNA, designed to increase translation of the remaining normal *JAG1* copy rather than replace DNA. A liver-targeted GalNAc-conjugated ASO raised Jag1 protein in *Jag1* heterozygous mice to above half of normal levels, improved bile-duct development, and reduced blood bile acids and triglycerides. This is a particularly relevant approach because most ALGS is caused by haploinsufficiency—having one working copy of a gene but not enough of its protein product. It remains an early animal-study result, with no published human dosing, safety, or efficacy data. [JAG1-upregulating ASO study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12446204/)

**AAV gene therapy aimed at a downstream developmental brake.** In a 2025 study, investigators used adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8) to deliver an RNA-silencing sequence against *Sox4*, a gene whose excess activity appeared to interfere with bile-duct formation in ALGS mouse models. A single postnatal treatment improved bile-duct architecture, cholestasis-related blood tests, inflammation, fibrosis, and longer-term liver outcomes; benefit was also reported when treatment was given after liver injury had appeared. This is not gene replacement or gene editing: it is a liver-directed gene-silencing strategy intended to enable bile-duct development despite reduced Jag1 signaling. [AAV-SOX4 mouse study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40446945/)

**Regeneration biology and Notch-pathway restoration.** A zebrafish ALGS model showed that intrahepatic bile ducts can regenerate after Jagged/Notch signaling resumes. The work identified progenitor cells in the extrahepatic bile duct—the duct outside the liver—as an important source of replacement intrahepatic cholangiocytes, the cells lining bile ducts. This finding provides a biological rationale for treatments that restore or amplify Notch signaling after birth, including gene-, RNA-, or small-molecule-based approaches, but it does not yet establish that the same degree of regeneration can be achieved safely in children. [Zebrafish bile-duct regeneration study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8844142/)

**Symptom-directed bile-acid transport inhibition.** The most clinically mature advances are oral ileal bile acid transporter inhibitors, which reduce recirculation of bile acids from the intestine to the liver. These drugs are major improvements in quality of life and may reduce the need for invasive symptom-control procedures in some patients, but they do not restore bile ducts, repair *JAG1* or *NOTCH2*, or address non-liver manifestations. Maralixibat received US approval in September 2021 for cholestatic itching associated with ALGS. [FDA 2021 novel drug approvals](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/novel-drug-approvals-fda/novel-drug-approvals-2021) [GeneReviews targeted therapy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1273/)

## Clinical Trials and Experimental Approaches

The completed Phase 3 ASSERT trial tested odevixibat against placebo in 52 patients with genetically confirmed ALGS and significant itching. At 24 weeks, odevixibat produced significantly greater improvements in caregiver-reported scratching and serum bile acids than placebo; diarrhea was more frequent with odevixibat, while no participant discontinued treatment and no deaths occurred during the treatment period. Albireo, now part of Ipsen, sponsored the trial. These results support symptom management rather than cure. [ASSERT Phase 3 results](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38670135/) [ASSERT trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04674761)

Maralixibat’s published Phase 2b ICONIC randomized-withdrawal trial found that children switched from maralixibat to placebo had worsening serum bile acids and itching, while those who continued treatment maintained benefit; improvements were reported through the open-label extension. Mirum Pharmaceuticals funded the study. [ICONIC Phase 2 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34755627/)

Longer-term follow-up remains active. Ipsen’s Phase 3 ASSERT-EXT study of odevixibat was listed as active but not recruiting as of July 2, 2026, with estimated completion on December 31, 2026; it has enrolled 62 participants and is evaluating longer-term safety and effectiveness. A separate Ipsen-sponsored registry, begun in 2025 and recruiting as of May 28, 2026, is following real-world odevixibat outcomes, including biliary-diversion and transplant-related outcomes. [ASSERT-EXT trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05035030) [Odevixibat real-world registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06850038)

## Methodologies and Scientific Approaches

Researchers are combining genetically engineered *Jag1* haploinsufficient mice, zebrafish models with impaired Jagged/Notch signaling, patient-derived cells, human liver tissue, and three-dimensional cellular systems. These models allow investigators to measure bile-duct number and branching, bile flow, liver inflammation and fibrosis, blood bile acids, survival, and gene-expression changes while testing whether developmental pathways can be restarted after birth. [Zebrafish bile-duct regeneration study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8844142/) [AAV-SOX4 mouse study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40446945/)

Advanced molecular approaches include liver-targeted GalNAc-ASOs to increase translation from the healthy *JAG1* allele, AAV vectors to silence disease-modifying genes such as *SOX4*, single-cell RNA sequencing to identify vulnerable liver-cell states, and functional assays that test whether individual *JAG1* and *NOTCH2* variants disrupt protein localization or signaling. [JAG1-upregulating ASO study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12446204/) [NIH-funded diagnostic research program](https://taggs.hhs.gov/Detail/AwardDetail?arg_AwardNum=R01DK134585&arg_ProgOfficeCode=119)

## Leading Institutions and Funding

Baylor College of Medicine and UMass Chan Medical School collaborated on the AAV-*Sox4* work, combining ALGS disease modeling, developmental biology, viral-vector expertise, and liver analysis. Arnatar Therapeutics conducted the published *JAG1*-upregulating ASO study; the paper reports that all authors were Arnatar employees and that a related patent application had been filed. Ipsen and Mirum Pharmaceuticals have driven the largest recent interventional clinical programs for odevixibat and maralixibat, respectively. [AAV-SOX4 mouse study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40446945/) [JAG1-upregulating ASO study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12446204/) [ASSERT Phase 3 results](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38670135/) [ICONIC Phase 2 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34755627/)

US government and patient-organization support also sustain the field. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases awarded Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia grant R01DK134585, “Resolving Uncertainty in Alagille Syndrome Diagnostics,” totaling $2.36 million across fiscal years 2023–2026, to improve variant interpretation and genotype-phenotype understanding. The Alagille Syndrome Alliance’s collaborative research grant is a two-year, $150,000 award, issued every two years, to support ALGS research. ## Strengths, Limitations, and Challenges

The central strength of recent research is mechanistic alignment: ASOs seek to increase the deficient JAG1 protein itself, while AAV-*Sox4* silencing and regenerative models target developmental barriers downstream of impaired Notch signaling. Importantly, the AAV study showed benefit in mice even after liver pathology had developed, which supports the possibility that postnatal intervention could matter. Meanwhile, bile-acid transporter inhibitors already provide validated biomarkers—itching scores and serum bile acids—and meaningful symptomatic benefit in patients. [JAG1-upregulating ASO study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12446204/) [AAV-SOX4 mouse study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40446945/) [ASSERT Phase 3 results](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38670135/)

The limitations are substantial. ALGS affects multiple organs during fetal and early-childhood development, so a liver-directed therapy may not repair existing heart, kidney, vascular, eye, or skeletal disease. Boosting JAG1/Notch signaling also demands careful dose control because Notch signaling is broadly active and excessive JAG1 expression could have unwanted effects. AAV treatment raises additional questions about immune responses, repeat dosing, long-term durability, age at treatment, and whether mouse bile-duct regeneration predicts human benefit. Finally, rare-disease trials are difficult to power, and access to specialized liver and transplant care remains uneven. [GeneReviews overview](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1273/) [JAG1-upregulating ASO study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12446204/) [AAV-SOX4 mouse study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40446945/)

## Outlook and Future Directions

As of August 8, 2026, a curative therapy for ALGS is not close to clinical availability, but the field has moved beyond symptom control toward biologically plausible attempts to rebuild or preserve the bile-duct system. The most important milestones to watch are replication of *JAG1*-upregulating ASO and AAV-*SOX4* results in additional models, full long-term results from bile-acid-inhibitor registries and extension studies, development of robust human organoid and patient-cell evidence, and—most importantly—an investigational-new-drug application or first-in-human trial for a disease-modifying genetic or RNA therapy.
